# Practical Clinical Guide: Direct Ophthalmoscopy
## Ocular Anatomy & Physiology &middot; Clinical Examination Protocol

---

## Clinical Procedure & Protocol


### 1. Purpose of the Procedure

Evaluate Ocular Health: To examine the health of the posterior segment of the eye (fundus), including the optic nerve head, retina, and blood vessels.

Assess Media Clarity: To check for opacities in the cornea, lens (e.g., cataracts), or vitreous using the red reflex.

Glaucoma Screening: To evaluate the optic disc and the cup-to-disc (C/D) ratio, which is critical for detecting glaucomatous damage.

Systemic Disease: To detect ocular signs of systemic diseases like hypertension (vascular changes) or diabetes.


### 2. Equipment Required

Monocular Direct Ophthalmoscope: An instrument providing an upright, magnified image (approx. 15x) of the fundus.

Distance Fixation Target: A non-accommodative target (e.g., a large letter "E" or spotlight) placed straight ahead or slightly upward.


### 3. Detailed Procedure


### Part A: Setup and Red Reflex

Patient Positioning: Seat the patient comfortably. Instruct them to remove their glasses (unless they have high astigmatism). Instruct the patient to look at a distant target (e.g., the top corner of the room or a fixation letter). This helps relax their accommodation and keeps the eye steady.

Instrument Setup: Turn on the ophthalmoscope light. Select the large round aperture (for dilated pupils) or the intermediate/small aperture (for undilated pupils) to reduce corneal reflection. Set the lens wheel to approximately +8.00 to +10.00 D (black or green numbers).


### The Approach (Red Reflex):

Hold the ophthalmoscope in your right hand and use your right eye to examine the patient’s right eye.

Position yourself about 10 to 15 cm from the patient and 15° to the temporal side of their line of sight.

Shine the light into the pupil. You should see an orange-red glow (the red reflex).

Observation: Look for any dark spots against the red glow. If spots move with your movement, they are posterior (e.g., posterior lens/vitreous); if they move against, they are anterior (e.g., cornea/anterior lens).


### Part B: Locating the Optic Disc

Moving In: Keep the red reflex in view. Slowly move toward the patient while reducing the plus power on the lens wheel (rotating toward zero or red numbers). Continue until your hand/knuckles gently touch the patient’s cheek.

Focusing: Adjust the lens wheel until the retinal vessels appear sharp. The final lens power depends on your refractive error and the patient's.

Finding the Disc: Approach from an angle of 15° temporal to the patient’s visual axis. If you see blood vessels but not the disc, follow the vessels. The vessels branch out like tree limbs; trace them back toward the thicker "trunk" to find the optic disc.


### Part C: Assessing the Optic Disc and Cup (C/D Ratio)

Evaluate the Disc Margins: Check if the edges of the disc are distinct (clear) or blurred. Normal margins are usually distinct.

Evaluate the Neuroretinal Rim (NRR): This is the pink tissue surrounding the white cup.

ISNT Rule: In a normal eye, the rim thickness usually follows the ISNT rule: Inferior is thickest, followed by Superior, Nasal, and Temporal is thinnest.

Assess the Optic Cup: Identify the central white depression (the cup).

Critical Tip: Do not rely only on color (pallor). Look for the "kinking" or bending of blood vessels as they cross the edge of the cup to determine the true cup margin.

Determine Cup-to-Disc Ratio: Estimate the decimal fraction of the disc occupied by the cup. Compare the vertical size of the cup to the vertical size of the disc.

Normal: 0.3 or less is typical.

Abnormal: Ratios >0.6, or a significant difference between the two eyes (>0.2 asymmetry), may suggest glaucoma.


### Part D: Vessels and Macula

Vessel Evaluation: Follow the superior and inferior vascular arcades. Note the Artery-to-Vein (A/V) Ratio. Arteries are brighter red and thinner; veins are darker and thicker. A normal ratio is about 2/3. Check for spontaneous venous pulsation (SVP) where veins exit the disc.

Macula Exam: Finally, ask the patient to look directly at the light (or move your light temporally). The macula is darker and avascular. Look for the bright foveal reflex (a pinpoint reflection) in young patients. Note that this may be uncomfortable for the patient, so do it quickly.


### 4. Method to Document (Recording)

Record findings for each eye (OD/OS) covering the "Four Ds": Disc, Diameter (Cup), Detailing (Vessels), and Defect (Macula/Background).

Media: Clear (or describe opacities like "cortical cataract").


### Disc:

Margins: Distinct, blurred, or elevated.

Color: Pink (healthy) or Pale (atrophy).

C/D Ratio: Record as a decimal (e.g., 0.3) for both Horizontal and Vertical dimensions if possible (e.g., 0.3/0.3).

Vessels: Record A/V ratio (e.g., 2/3) and any abnormalities (e.g., "tortuous," "nipping").

Macula: "Clear," "Foveal reflex present (+FR)," or describe defects (e.g., "drusen," "pigment mottling").


### Example of Normal Recording:


### Direct Ophthalmoscopy:


### Media: Clear OD/OS.


### Disc: Margins distinct, pink rim. C/D 0.3 V/H.


### Vessels: A/V 2/3, no crossings changes. (+) SVP.


### Macula: Flat, clear, (+) foveal reflex.


### Example of Abnormal Recording:


### Direct Ophthalmoscopy:

Disc: Margins distinct. C/D 0.7 V / 0.6 H OD (vertical elongation).


### Vessels: A/V 1/2, mild tortuosity.


### Macula: Drusen noted.
