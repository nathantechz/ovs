# Practical Clinical Guide: The Cover Tests (Cover-Uncover & Alternate)
## Strabismus &middot; Clinical Examination Protocol

---

## Clinical Procedure & Protocol

Equipment Needed: An occluder (opaque or semi-opaque) and detailed fixation targets (distance and near). A prism bar (for the Prism Cover Test).


### I. Preparation & Target Selection

Lighting & Posture: Ensure the room illumination is high and the patient is well-lit, but avoid causing glare.

Refractive Correction: The patient should wear their habitual refractive correction for the distance being tested.

Target Choice: Use a letter from the line above the worst eye’s visual acuity. If the visual acuity is worse than 6/60, a spotlight can be used. Make sure the target is detailed enough to ensure fixation is easily maintained.

II. Procedure 1: The Cover Test (Detecting Manifest Strabismus/Tropia)

If you suspect a strabismus in one eye, cover the "good" eye first to observe the purest orthoptic state.

Watch the eye carefully as the cover approaches; a movement seen before the cover reaches the eye may indicate a dissociated vertical deviation (DVD).

As the cover moves over the eye, watch the uncovered eye. If the uncovered eye moves to take up fixation, a manifest strabismus (heterotropia) is present.

Hold the cover in place long enough to get a good result—recommendations range from 1–2 seconds up to 4–5 seconds.

III. Procedure 2: The Uncover Test (Detecting Latent Deviation/Heterophoria)

Slowly remove the cover and observe the eye that was just covered.

Remove the cover vertically (upwards); this prevents you from accidentally covering your own eye and blocking your observation.

If a movement occurs as the eye regains binocular fixation, a heterophoria is present.

Grade the quality of the recovery movement, which is a great predictor of whether a phoria is decompensating (causing symptoms). Grade it on a scale of 1 to 5:


### Grade 1: Rapid and smooth.


### Grade 2: Slightly slow/jerky.


### Grade 3: Definitely slow/jerky but not breaking down.

Grade 4: Slow/jerky and breaks down with repeat covering, or only recovers after a blink.


### Grade 5: Breaks down readily after 1–3 covers.

IV. Procedure 3: The Alternating Cover Test (Revealing the Total Deviation)

After the cover/uncover test, alternate the cover from one eye to the other for about 6 further covers to break down fusion completely.

Observe how the angle changes or "builds" towards the total angle.

As the angle builds, it often becomes much easier to detect smaller vertical deviations.


### V. Clinical Value & Estimating Amplitude

Visual Estimation: You can accurately estimate the size of the deviation without prisms by watching the amplitude of the refixation saccade:

At 6 meters, the 6/12 Snellen line is about 12 cm long. An eye jumping from one end of this line to the other equals an angular movement of 2 prism diopters (2Δ).

At near (1/3 meter), using a centimeter ruler as a target, a jump from the 1 cm mark to the 2 cm mark equates to 3Δ.

Prism Cover Test: To objectively measure the angle, place a prism bar over the deviating eye and alter the power during the alternate cover test until no movement is seen.


### VI. Advanced Clinical Pearls

Amblyopia delay: If amblyopia is suspected, hold the cover over the good eye a little longer, as the amblyopic eye takes more time to take up fixation.

The Microtropia Trap: Do not assume there is no strabismus just because you see no movement on the cover test. A microtropia with identity will show absolutely no movement because the angle of the strabismus perfectly matches the angle of their eccentric fixation.

Prism Stacking Warning: When using loose prisms to measure large angles, do not stack prisms with bases in the same direction (e.g., holding a 40Δ and a 5Δ together). They do not add arithmetically and will result in significant measurement errors (e.g., 40Δ+5Δ≈58Δ).

Semi-Opaque Occluders: Consider using a semi-opaque occluder. This suspends the patient's normal binocular vision (they see worse than 6/60 through it), but allows the practitioner to actually see what the occluded eye is doing behind the cover.
