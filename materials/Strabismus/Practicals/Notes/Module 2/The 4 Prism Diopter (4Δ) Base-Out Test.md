# Practical Clinical Guide: The 4 Prism Diopter (4Δ) Base-Out Test
## Strabismus &middot; Clinical Examination Protocol

---

## Clinical Procedure & Protocol

Equipment Needed: A 4Δ loose prism and a highly specific fixation target (a single, isolated dot on a blank, featureless background, such as a bare wall or a blank sheet of A4 paper).


### I. Purpose and Principles

Clinical Value: This is an objective test designed to detect the presence of a small central suppression scotoma, which is a hallmark of microtropia (small angle strabismus). A major advantage is that it does not rely on the patient's subjective responses.

Physiological Basis: Placing a 4Δ base-out prism in front of a normal eye shifts the retinal image nasally. According to Hering's Law of Equal Innervation, this normally triggers a conjugate version movement of both eyes (away from the apex of the prism), followed immediately by a symmetric fusional vergence movement (adduction) to regain binocular single vision. However, if the prism shifts the image entirely within a suppression scotoma, the brain does not perceive the shift, and the normal refixation reflex is not triggered.


### II. Crucial Preparation: Target Selection

The Snellen Chart Trap: You must not use a standard letter chart for this test. If a patient looks at a letter on a Snellen chart, the 4Δ prism displaces the entire chart across the retina, not just the fixation letter. The peripheral retinal stimulation from the surrounding letters may trigger an eye movement even if a central suppression scotoma exists, leading to a false-negative result. An isolated target on a uniform field is mandatory.

III. Procedure & Interpretation Instruct the patient to fixate steadily on the isolated target. The test involves two distinct phases:


### Phase 1: Prism over the Non-Strabismic (Fixing) Eye

Procedure: Drop the 4Δ base-out prism in front of the normal eye. Observe the fellow (suspected microtropic) eye.

Normal Response: Both eyes make a rapid version movement, followed by a corrective vergence movement of the eye without the prism.

Microtropia Response: Both eyes make the initial conjugate version movement (because the normal eye sees the image shift), but the microtropic eye fails to make the corrective vergence movement because the target has now landed in its suppression scotoma.


### Phase 2: Prism over the Suspected Microtropic Eye

Procedure: Remove the prism, allow the patient to regain fixation, then drop the 4Δ base-out prism in front of the suspected microtropic eye. Observe both eyes.

Normal Response: The eye with the prism makes a vergence movement while the other eye maintains fixation (or the standard version-vergence sequence occurs).

Microtropia Response: Because the image is displaced purely within the central suppression area of the affected eye, the brain does not register the shift. No movement of either eye will take place.


### IV. Advanced Clinical Pearls

Microtropia vs. Organic Amblyopia: A central scotoma caused by macular pathology (organic amblyopia) will also cause a lack of movement on this test. To differentiate the two, occlude the normal eye and repeat the test monocularly over the affected eye.

If it is a microtropic suppression scotoma, the suppression lifts under monocular conditions, and the eye will make a version movement to find the target.

If it is a pathological central scotoma, the eye will still fail to make a saccadic movement even under monocular conditions.

Test Limitations: Proceed with caution and do not base a diagnosis of microtropia purely on this test. Test-retest reliability can be low, and atypical responses frequently occur even in normal patients. It is best used as part of a battery of tests (such as combining it with the Bagolini striated lens test and visuoscopy)
