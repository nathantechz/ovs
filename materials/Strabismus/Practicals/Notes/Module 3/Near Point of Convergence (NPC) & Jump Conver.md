# Practical Clinical Guide: Near Point of Convergence (NPC) & Jump Convergence
## Strabismus &middot; Clinical Examination Protocol

---

## Clinical Procedure & Protocol

Equipment Needed: A detailed accommodative near target (such as the tip of a pencil, a 6/9 equivalent letter, or a small picture for children). A measurement tool, such as an RAF rule or a retractable tape measure. Optional: A pen torch and a red filter.


### I. Purpose and Principles

Clinical Value: The NPC test assesses the absolute limit of the patient's ability to converge their eyes while maintaining binocular single vision. It is the primary test for diagnosing convergence insufficiency, which is a highly common cause of visually provoked asthenopia (reading strain) and headaches.

Normal Values: A normal NPC is approximately 6 to 8 cm. A measurement over 10 cm is generally considered low or insufficient.


### II. Target Selection & Preparation

Accommodative Target: It is generally recommended to use an accommodative target (like a small letter) rather than a simple penlight, as this requires the patient to maintain precise focus, thereby fully engaging the accommodative convergence mechanism.

The Proximal Effect: While the RAF rule is a standard instrument, resting it firmly against the patient's cheeks can artificially increase the patient's awareness of the target's nearness (proximal convergence). Using a simple near card and a free-space tape measure can eliminate this artefact.


### III. Procedure (The "Push-Up" Method)

Hold the target at approximately 50 cm away from the patient in the midline.

Instruct the patient to fixate intently on the detailed target and to report as soon as it appears double or if they feel significant visual discomfort.

Slowly move the target towards the patient's nose.

Observe the patient's eyes carefully. They should turn inward symmetrically, and the pupils should constrict.

Stop when either the patient reports diplopia or you observe an eye lose fixation. Measure this distance in centimetres.


### IV. Clinical Observation & Interpretation

The Subjective Break: The point at which the patient reports seeing double (diplopia).

The Objective Break: The point at which the examiner observes one eye lose fixation and drift outward. The eye that diverges is typically the non-dominant eye.

Diagnosing Suppression: If the examiner sees the objective break (an eye turns out) but the patient does not report diplopia, or if the patient reports that the target suddenly "jumps" to one side, this indicates central suppression at the break point. Any difference between the objective and subjective break points indicates decompensation and suppression.

Discomfort vs. Diplopia: Ask the patient when visual discomfort is first noticed, as this distance may be more functionally relevant to their daily symptoms than the absolute break point.


### V. Advanced Clinical Pearls

The Fatigue Factor (Rule of Three): The NPC should be measured at least three times. In a normal patient, learning effects may slightly improve the score. If the NPC repeatedly worsens on successive attempts, this indicates ill-sustained convergence or fatigue, pointing towards poor fusional reserves.

Capobianco’s Variation: To enhance the detection of foveal suppression and make the objective break easier to see, use a pen torch as the target and place a red filter over the patient's dominant eye. At the break point, the patient should see one red and one white light. If they only see one, suppression is confirmed.

Testing "Jump" Convergence: The push-up method tests convergence amplitude, but "jump" convergence tests the stamina and facility (speed of response) of the vergence system.

Procedure: Have the patient fixate on a distance target (e.g., a letter at 6 metres). Hold a near target a few centimetres beyond their measured push-up NPC. Ask them to rapidly shift fixation from the distance to the near target.

Interpretation: Normal subjects make a single, smooth vergence movement. Patients with poor convergence will make several jerky adjustments, may experience transient diplopia, or will physically pull their head back to increase the viewing distance.

Differentiating Accommodative Issues: If a patient reports blur or diplopia during the push-up test, briefly cover each eye in turn and ask if the target becomes clear. This helps differentiate a pure convergence problem from an accommodative failure
