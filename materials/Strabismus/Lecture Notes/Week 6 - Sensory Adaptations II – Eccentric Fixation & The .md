## I. Eccentric Fixation (EF)

## 1. Definition and Context

- **Definition:**  Eccentric fixation is a monocular sensory phenomenon, often found in strabismic amblyopia, where the eye uses a non-foveal area of the retina for fixation even when the dominant eye is covered,.
- **Appearance:**  Because the patient is fixing with a non-foveal point, the eye may appear to be looking away from the object of regard, even though they feel they are looking directly at it.
- **Mechanism:**  It represents a loss of the principal visual direction of the fovea. The acuity at the eccentric point is reduced compared to the fovea, limiting the potential visual acuity of that eye.
- **Contrast with Eccentric Viewing:**  While the texts focus on *Eccentric Fixation*  as an adaptation to amblyopia/strabismus (often unconscious), *Eccentric Viewing*  generally refers to the use of peripheral retina to see around a central scotoma (e.g., in macular disease) or as a conscious strategy. In eccentric fixation associated with microtropia, the angle of eccentricity may equal the angle of the strabismus.

## 2. Diagnosis: Visuoscopy (Ophthalmoscopy)

The most popular and accurate technique for detecting eccentric fixation is using a direct ophthalmoscope (Visuoscopy).

- **Procedure:**
- Occlude the eye not being tested (usually the better eye first to demonstrate the test).
- Use a red-free filter (green light) to increase comfort and visibility of landmarks.
- Project the ophthalmoscope's fixation target (e.g., a star or graticule) onto the fundus.
- Ask the patient to look directly at the star/target.
- Observe the position of the foveal reflex relative to the target.
- **Classification of Fixation:**  If the foveal reflex is not centered on the target, EF is present. It should be recorded based on:
- **Position:**  Superior, Inferior, Nasal, or Temporal (relative to the fovea). In esotropia, EF is usually nasal.
- **Magnitude:**  Estimated in degrees or prism diopters (1^\circ \approx 1.75\Delta - 2\Delta) from the fovea.
- **Stability:**  Classified as **steady**  or **unsteady** . Fixation in amblyopic eyes is generally unsteady.

## II. The 4 Prism Diopter (4\Delta) Base-Out Test

This objective test is used to detect small central suppression scotomas, particularly in cases of **microtropia**  (small angle strabismus <10\Delta) or suspected small angle squints where the cover test is inconclusive,.

## 1. Theory

- In a normal eye, placing a base-out prism shifts the image onto the temporal retina. This stimulates a refixation reflex: the eye moves in (adducts) to place the image back on the fovea.
- In a microtropic eye (or an eye with a central scotoma), a suppression scotoma exists at the fovea/zero point. If the prism shifts the image *within*  this suppression area, the eye will not perceive the movement and will make no effort to refixate,.

## 2. Procedure

- **Target:**  Use an isolated detailed target (to prevent peripheral fusion cues) at distance or near,.
- **Step 1 (Prism over Normal Eye):**  Place the 4\Delta Base-Out prism over the fixing (normal) eye.
- *Result:*  Both eyes should move in the direction of the apex (version movement) because the fixing eye refixates. Then, the non-fixing eye should make a slow fusional vergence movement back out (re-alignment),.
- *Microtropia Finding:*  If the non-fixing eye has a central suppression scotoma, it will make the initial version movement (following the good eye) but **fail**  to make the corrective vergence movement,.
- **Step 2 (Prism over Suspected Eye):**  Place the 4\Delta Base-Out prism over the suspected microtropic/amblyopic eye.
- *Result:*  If a central suppression scotoma is present, the image shift occurs within the scotoma. The patient does not see the image move, so **no eye movement**  is observed in either eye,.

## 3. Limitations

- **Atypical Responses:**  Normal subjects can sometimes show atypical responses, and test-retest reliability can be low.
- **Target Selection:**  Using a crowded chart (like a whole line of letters) can cause artifacts because peripheral retinal stimulation might trigger a movement even if a central scotoma exists,. An isolated target is essential.

## III. Microtropia Context

## 1. Definition

- A small angle strabismus (less than 10\Delta, usually <6\Delta),.
- Often associated with amblyopia, eccentric fixation, and Harmonious Anomalous Retinal Correspondence (HARC),.

## 2. Microtropia "With Identity"

- A unique condition where the angle of the strabismus is exactly equal to the angle of eccentric fixation.
- **Cover Test Consequence:**  Because the eccentric point is used for monocular fixation *and*  binocular correspondence, the eye does **not move**  on the cover test,.
- **Diagnosis:**  Requires Visuoscopy (to find EF) and the 4\Delta Base-Out test (to find suppression).

## 3. Microtropia "Without Identity"

- The angle of eccentric fixation is less than the angle of strabismus.
- **Cover Test:**  A small movement may be seen on the unilateral cover test.
