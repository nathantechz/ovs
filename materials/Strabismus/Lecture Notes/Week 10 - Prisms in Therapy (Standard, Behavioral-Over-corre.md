## I. Standard Use: Relieving Diplopia and Compensatory Prisms

## 1. Principles of Prescribing

- **Goal:**  The primary use of standard prisms is to relieve symptoms (diplopia or asthenopia) by optically moving the image to a position where the eyes can maintain fusion comfortably.
- **Indications:**
- **Decompensated Heterophoria:**  Prisms are often used when exercises are not possible or have failed, particularly in the elderly. Base-In (BI) prisms are commonly used for decompensated exophoria or convergence insufficiency in older patients.
- **Vertical Deviations:**  Small vertical heterophorias respond well to prismatic correction. Even small amounts of vertical prism (determined via fixation disparity testing) can be extremely effective in restoring compensation,.
- **Incomitant Deviations:**  In long-standing incomitant strabismus (e.g., paresis), prisms can extend the field of single binocular vision. The prism is usually prescribed to correct the deviation in the **primary position** ,.

## 2. Methods of Determination

- **Fixation Disparity (Mallett Unit):**  The "aligning prism" is the minimum prism required to eliminate a fixation disparity (align the nonius strips). This is independent of the size of the heterophoria and is a measure of the patient's ability to compensate,.
- *Procedure:*  Introduce prism (e.g., in 0.5 or 1 Δ steps) until the strips align. Prescribe the minimum amount necessary to eliminate the slip,.
- **Criteria:**
- **Sheard’s Criterion:**  States that the opposing fusional reserve (to blur) should be at least twice the size of the heterophoria. Prisms are prescribed to meet this ratio,.
- **Percival’s Criterion:**  States that the fixation point should fall within the middle third of the fusion range. This is often useful for esophoria,.

## 3. Adaptation Considerations

- **Prism Adaptation:**  Patients with normal binocular vision often "eat up" prisms (adapt to them), meaning the phoria increases back to its original level with the prism in place. However, patients with **abnormal**  binocular vision (symptoms) usually do **not**  adapt to prisms.
- **Clinical Test:**  It is prudent to leave the proposed prism in place for 3–5 minutes (or have the patient read) to ensure the correction maintains its effect and the patient does not adapt to it before prescribing,.

## II. Behavioral/Active Use: Over-Correcting and Adverse Prisms

While standard prisms passively correct a deviation, "adverse" or "over-correcting" prisms are used therapeutically to disrupt sensory adaptations like **Harmonious Anomalous Retinal Correspondence (HARC)** .

## 1. Adverse Prisms (Mallett’s Technique)

- **Concept:**  Use a prism strength that opposes the deviation (e.g., Base-In for Esotropia) to actively break down anomalous correspondence.
- **Mechanism:**  In convergent strabismus, a strong Base-In prism (e.g., 16\Delta) moves the image to a retinal point that forces the eye to diverge or breaks the sensory adaptation because the disparity is too large to be overcome by the anomalous system. This produces a rapid breakdown of HARC.
- **Contraindications:**  This produces diplopia (double vision). It is inappropriate for patients who drive or operate machinery due to safety risks.

## 2. Over-Correcting Prisms

- **Concept:**  This technique involves prescribing a prism that exceeds the angle of deviation, converting an esotropia into a consecutive exotropia (or vice versa).
- **Application:**  For a convergent strabismus, relieving prism is added until the cover test shows a divergent strabismus (overcorrection).
- **Goal:**  To stimulate the fovea or break established suppression/ARC patterns. Approximately 8–10 Δ of overcorrection is typically required.
- **Note:**  This aligns with "Greenwald’s" behavioral concepts mentioned in the syllabus, utilizing prisms not to compensate, but to disrupt stable pathological adaptations and force the visual system to reorganize.

## III. The Prism Adaptation Test (PAT)

The PAT is a diagnostic and prognostic tool used primarily in acquired esotropia to determine the true angle of deviation and the potential for binocular single vision (BSV) prior to surgery.

## 1. Indications

- Used in patients with **acquired esotropia**  or large constant deviations where routine cover testing is inconclusive or where the angle appears variable.
- Used to identify patients who are "prism responders" (have fusion potential) versus "non-responders" (have ARC).

## 2. Procedure

- **Initial Prism:**  Fresnel prisms are applied to the patient's spectacles to fully correct or slightly over-correct the deviation.
- **Wear Time:**  The patient wears the prisms for a period (e.g., 1 week).
- **Re-evaluation:**  The patient is re-examined. If the eyes have re-converged (the deviation has increased/manifested through the prism), the prism strength is increased.
- **Endpoint:**  This process continues until the deviation is stable (<8\Delta) or the prism power exceeds 50\Delta,.

## 3. Interpretation

- **Prism Responder (Good Prognosis):**  The visual axes remain straight with the prisms, and **Binocular Single Vision (BSV)**  is demonstrated (e.g., via Bagolini lenses).
- *Management:*  Surgery is performed to correct the **maximum angle**  defined by the prisms.
- **Non-Responder ("Eating Up" Prism):**  The eyes re-converge to the original angle despite the prism. This indicates **Anomalous Retinal Correspondence (ARC)**  or a lack of fusion potential. The patient "eats up" the prism to maintain their anomalous sensory adaptation.
- *Management:*  Surgery is generally based on the **original**  (smaller) angle of deviation, as the goal is cosmetic rather than functional fusion.
