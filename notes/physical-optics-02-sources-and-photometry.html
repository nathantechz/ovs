Physical Optics

Sources of Light, Radiometry & Photometry

The electromagnetic spectrum, solid angle, the radiometric and photometric unit families, photopic and scotopic efficiency curves, the inverse square and cosine laws, and retinal illuminance in trolands.

1. The electromagnetic spectrum

All electromagnetic radiation is the same phenomenon differing only in frequency. The visible band is a narrow slice, roughly 380–780 nm — under one octave, while hearing spans about ten.

2. Radiometry vs photometry — the key distinction

This distinction underlies everything that follows:

  • Radiometry measures radiant energy in physical units. It treats all wavelengths equally. 1 W of 400 nm equals 1 W of 555 nm.
  • Photometry measures the same radiation as weighted by the human visual system. 1 W at 555 nm produces far more visual sensation than 1 W at 400 nm, and photometry accounts for that.
In one line Photometry is radiometry multiplied by the eye's spectral sensitivity curve. Every photometric quantity has a radiometric twin; the bridge between them is V(λ).

3. Solid angle — the steradian

A plane angle is arc over radius (radians). A solid angle extends this into three dimensions: area on a sphere over radius squared, in steradians (sr).

Ω = A / r² A = area on the sphere's surface · r = radius

A full sphere has surface area 4πr², so it subtends 4π ≈ 12.57 sr. A hemisphere subtends 2π sr. The steradian is dimensionless — a ratio of area to area — which is why it can appear and disappear in unit definitions without breaking them.

5. Photopic and scotopic efficiency curves

The luminous efficiency function V(λ) describes how effectively each wavelength produces visual sensation. There are two, because the retina has two receptor systems.

The Purkinje shift

As illumination falls and vision moves from cones to rods, peak sensitivity shifts toward the blue end. In daylight a red flower may look brighter than a blue one; at dusk the blue appears brighter, though neither has changed. This is the Purkinje shift, and it is a direct consequence of the two curves peaking at different wavelengths.

Luminous efficacy vs efficiency

These two terms are examined often and confused often:

  • Luminous efficacy — lumens produced per watt, in lm/W. Maximum is 683 lm/W, at 555 nm photopic.
  • Luminous efficiency — efficacy expressed as a fraction of that maximum. Dimensionless, 0 to 1. This is V(λ) itself.
Φv = 683 · ∫ Φe(λ) · V(λ) dλ converts radiant flux (W) into luminous flux (lm)

6. The photometric units

The candela is the SI base unit — the only base unit defined in terms of human perception. One candela is a source emitting 1/683 W/sr at 540 THz (555 nm).

Illuminance vs luminance Illuminance (lux) is light falling on a surface — what a light meter reads at the desk. Luminance (cd/m²) is light coming from a surface toward the eye — what determines perceived brightness. A white page and a black page under the same lamp share illuminance but differ greatly in luminance.
Clinical link Standards are written in these units: chart luminance 80–320 cd/m² for visual acuity testing, consulting room illuminance around 500 lux, and background luminance 10 cd/m² for standard automated perimetry.

7. The inverse square law

Light from a point source spreads over a sphere whose area grows as r². The same flux over a larger area means illuminance falls as the square of distance:

E = I / d² E in lux · I in candela · d in metres

Double the distance and illuminance drops to one quarter. Triple it and it drops to one ninth.

Conditions The law holds only for a point source (in practice, distance greater than about five times the source diameter) and a non-absorbing medium. It does not apply to collimated laser beams or to extended sources at close range.

8. Lambert's cosine law

The inverse square law assumes light strikes the surface perpendicularly. When it arrives at an angle θ from the normal, the same flux spreads over a larger area, reducing illuminance by cos θ:

E = (I / d²) · cos θ the combined inverse-square and cosine law

Lambertian surfaces

Lambert's name also attaches to a second idea. A Lambertian (perfectly diffusing) surface has the same luminance from every viewing angle — matte paper, magnesium oxide, a projection screen. Its radiated intensity varies as cos θ, but the visible area shrinks by the same cos θ, so the ratio stays constant and brightness looks unchanged as you move around it.

9. Retinal illuminance and the troland

Neither illuminance nor luminance tells you how much light actually reaches the retina, because that depends on pupil size. A scene at fixed luminance delivers far more light to the retina through an 8 mm dilated pupil than through a 2 mm constricted one.

The troland (td) was defined to capture exactly this:

T = L × p T = retinal illuminance (trolands) · L = luminance (cd/m²) · p = pupil area (mm²)

One troland is the retinal illuminance from a surface of 1 cd/m² viewed through a pupil of 1 mm². Since pupil area is πr² = πd²/4, a 4 mm pupil gives p ≈ 12.57 mm².

Worked example

A patient views a chart of luminance 160 cd/m² with a pupil diameter of 4 mm.

  • Pupil area: p = π × 4² / 4 = 12.57 mm²
  • Retinal illuminance: T = 160 × 12.57 = ≈ 2011 trolands

If a mydriatic dilates the pupil to 8 mm, the area quadruples to 50.3 mm² and retinal illuminance rises to about 8042 td — four times as much light on the retina from an unchanged chart. This is why dilated patients are given sunglasses.

Photopic and scotopic trolands Because the two efficiency curves differ, trolands must be specified as photopic or scotopic. Comparing values computed under different curves is a common examination error.
