Physical Optics

The Nature of Light

Light as an electromagnetic oscillation — the wave equation, simple harmonic motion, the transverse nature of the oscillation, and the four quantities that describe any wave: frequency, wavelength, amplitude and phase.

1. Light as an electromagnetic oscillation

Light is not a substance travelling through space. It is a disturbance in the electromagnetic field — a pattern of oscillating electric and magnetic fields that regenerate each other as they move.

Maxwell's insight was that the two fields sustain one another:

  • A changing electric field creates a magnetic field.
  • A changing magnetic field creates an electric field.

Once started, neither field needs a medium to keep going — each one continuously regenerates the other. This is why light crosses the vacuum of space, and why the old idea of a "luminiferous aether" turned out to be unnecessary.

In a light wave the two fields are locked in a fixed geometry:

  • The electric field E and magnetic field B are perpendicular to each other.
  • Both are perpendicular to the direction of travel.
  • They oscillate in phase — peaking and crossing zero together.

In vacuum both fields travel at c = 3.00 × 108 m/s. Maxwell derived this speed from purely electrical and magnetic constants, and it matched the measured speed of light — which is how we know light is an electromagnetic wave.

2. The wave equation

Any disturbance that propagates without changing shape obeys the wave equation. For the electric field of light travelling along z:

∂²E/∂z² = (1/v²) · ∂²E/∂t² where v is the speed of the wave in the medium

You will rarely solve this in clinical practice, but its solution is the equation you use constantly. For a wave of a single frequency:

E(z, t) = E0 sin(kz − ωt + φ) E0 = amplitude · k = 2π/λ (wave number) · ω = 2πf (angular frequency) · φ = initial phase

Read it this way: the field at any point is a sine wave in time, and at any instant it is a sine wave in space. That single sentence is the whole content of the equation.

3. Sinusoidal and simple harmonic oscillation

Why a sine wave specifically? Because light is generated by charges undergoing simple harmonic motion (SHM), and SHM always produces a sinusoid.

A system performs SHM when the restoring force is proportional to the displacement:

F = −kx  →  x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ) the minus sign means the force always pulls back toward equilibrium

An electron displaced within an atom feels exactly this kind of restoring force. It oscillates sinusoidally, and an accelerating charge radiates — so the emitted field is also sinusoidal. The sine wave is not an approximation chosen for convenience; it is what the physics produces.

Real light sources emit in short bursts called wave trains (typically ~10-8 s from a thermal source). A perfectly infinite sine wave is an idealisation — and the finite length of real wave trains is precisely what limits coherence, which you will meet in the interference topics.

4. The transverse nature of the oscillation

Light is a transverse wave: the oscillation is perpendicular to the direction of travel. Sound, by contrast, is longitudinal — air molecules move back and forth along the direction of travel.

Why this matters Polarisation is the proof of transversality. A longitudinal wave has no "sideways" direction to distinguish, so it cannot be polarised. The fact that light can be polarised — and that Polaroid filters work at all — is direct experimental evidence that its oscillation is transverse.
Clinical link Every polarising tool in the clinic depends on this: polarised sunglasses cutting horizontally-polarised glare from roads and water, the polarised strips in stereoacuity tests (Titmus, Randot), and polarised duochrome and binocular balance techniques. All of them exist only because light oscillates transversely.

5. Frequency, wavelength, amplitude and phase

Frequency (f)

Oscillations per second, in hertz (Hz). Frequency is set by the source and never changes when light enters a new medium. Visible light spans roughly 4.3 × 1014 Hz (red) to 7.5 × 1014 Hz (violet). Colour follows frequency, not wavelength.

Wavelength (λ)

The distance between successive corresponding points, in nanometres for visible light (~380–780 nm). Wavelength does change with medium:

v = fλ   and   n = c/v  →  λmedium = λvacuum / n f stays fixed, v drops, so λ must shorten

In the eye this is not a footnote. With the vitreous at n ≈ 1.336, a 555 nm ray travels at about 415 nm inside the eye.

Amplitude (A)

The maximum displacement of the field. What the eye perceives as brightness is intensity, and intensity goes as the square of amplitude:

I ∝ A² double the amplitude → four times the intensity

Phase (φ)

Where a wave sits in its cycle, in degrees or radians. Phase has no meaning for a single wave in isolation — it matters only when two waves are compared:

  • In phase (0°, 360°): crests align → constructive interference.
  • Antiphase (180°): crest meets trough → destructive interference.
Clinical link Controlled phase difference is what makes anti-reflection coatings work. A coating one quarter-wavelength thick returns the second reflection 180° out of phase with the first, so the two cancel and the transmitted light increases — the reason coated spectacle lenses look clearer and photograph without ghost reflections.
