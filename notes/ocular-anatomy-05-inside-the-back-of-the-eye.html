Inside the Back of the Eye

This week, we are looking at the Posterior Segment, which is the large back part of the eye. This is where the magic of seeing actually happens!

1. The Vitreous: The Clear Jelly

The biggest space inside the eye, right behind the lens, is filled with a clear, squishy gel called the Vitreous.

  • What is it? It is made of 99% water and helps the eyeball keep its nice, round shape.
  • Floaters and Flashes: As people get older, this jelly can shrink a little bit. Sometimes, tiny clumps form inside it. When you look at a bright blue sky or a blank white wall and see little dots, "bugs," or cobwebs drifting across your vision, those are called floaters. If the jelly tugs on the back of the eye, you might see quick sparks of light called flashes.

2. The Choroid: The Blood Supply

Remember the middle layer of the eye that feeds the eye parts? In the back of the eye, this layer is called the Choroid.

  • Its Job: It is packed tightly with tiny blood vessels. Its main job is to bring food and oxygen to the retina so it can do its hard work.

3. The Retina: The Camera Film

The Retina is a very thin, delicate skin that lines the inside of the back of the eye like wallpaper. It acts just like the film in a camera. It catches the light coming into your eye and turns it into electrical signals to send to your brain.

  • The Light Catchers (Photoreceptors): The retina is filled with millions of special cells that catch the light. There are two types:
  • Rods: There are about 130 million of these! They are amazing at helping us see in the dark (night vision) and letting us see things out of the corners of our eyes (peripheral vision). However, rods cannot see colors.
  • Cones: There are about 6 million of these. They need bright daylight to work. Cones are the superstars that let us see all the beautiful colors in the world and help us see tiny, sharp details.

4. Important Spots on the Retina

Doctors have special names for different areas of the retina:

  • The Macula: This is a special, tiny circle right in the center of the retina. It is packed with cone cells and gives us our straight-ahead, detailed vision (the vision you use to read this page).
  • The Fovea: This is the absolute center of the macula, like the exact bullseye on a dartboard. It gives us our very sharpest, clearest vision.
  • The Ora Serrata: This is just a fancy name for the jagged front edge of the retina where it stops.
Reference: Ocular Anatomy and Physiology, Sheila Coyne Nemeth & Al Lens (2nd Edition)
