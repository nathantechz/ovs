Nystagmus and Complex Cases

Course Module 4: Management and Therapy

I. Classification and Characteristics of Nystagmus

Nystagmus is defined as a regular, repetitive, involuntary movement of the eyes. It is broadly classified by age of onset (Congenital vs. Acquired) and by the conditions that elicit it (Physiological vs. Pathological).

1. Congenital (Infantile) Nystagmus (CN/IN)

  • Onset: Typically presents within the first 6 months of life.
  • Sensory vs. Motor:
  • Sensory Defect Nystagmus: Caused by early visual deprivation prevents the fixation reflex from developing (e.g., albinism, achromatopsia, congenital cataracts, optic nerve hypoplasia).
  • Motor Defect Nystagmus: Primary defect in the efferent motor pathway; the eyes are structurally normal.
  • Oscillopsia: A key distinguishing feature is that patients with congenital nystagmus generally do not experience oscillopsia (the illusory sensation that the stationary world is moving), whereas patients with acquired nystagmus do.
  • Waveforms: Can be pendular (velocity equal in both directions) or jerk (slow drift followed by a fast corrective saccade).
  • The Null Zone: This is a specific position of gaze where the nystagmus intensity (amplitude/frequency) is minimal, and visual acuity is maximal.
  • Compensatory Head Posture (CHP): Patients often adopt a head turn or tilt to place their eyes in the null zone while looking straight ahead.

2. Acquired Nystagmus

  • Etiology: Associated with neurological conditions (e.g., Multiple Sclerosis, brainstem or cerebellar lesions) or toxicity.
  • Symptoms: Unlike CN, acquired nystagmus is frequently associated with oscillopsia, vertigo, and nausea.
  • Superior Oblique Myokymia: A specific form of acquired, intermittent, monocular nystagmus involving the superior oblique muscle, often causing vertical/torsional diplopia and oscillopsia.

3. Latent Nystagmus (LN) and Infantile Esotropia

  • Definition: A jerk nystagmus that appears or greatly increases in intensity only when one eye is covered (monocular viewing).
  • Direction: The fast phase (beat) is always directed toward the fixating eye (e.g., if the right eye is covered, the left eye beats left).
  • Association with Strabismus: Latent nystagmus is a classic sign of Infantile Esotropia Syndrome (Congenital Esotropia). It suggests an early disruption of binocularity and is often found alongside Dissociated Vertical Deviation (DVD).
  • Clinical Implication: When measuring visual acuity in these patients, occluding one eye will induce nystagmus and reduce vision. Therefore, acuity should be measured with both eyes open, or by using a high-plus lens to blur (fog) the non-tested eye without blocking light.

II. Management Strategies

Therapy for nystagmus aims to reduce the intensity of the oscillation (thereby increasing foveation time) or to shift the null zone to the primary position to correct a head turn.

1. Optical Management

  • Refractive Correction: Essential to maximize visual potential.
  • Contact Lenses: Often superior to spectacles for nystagmus patients.
  • Mechanism: Contacts move with the eye, ensuring the patient is always looking through the optical center. They may also provide tactile feedback to the eyelids that helps dampen the nystagmus.
  • Prisms:
  • Null Zone Shifting: Prisms can be used to shift the image so the eyes align with the null zone while the head remains straight. This is useful for treating compensatory head postures.
  • Inducing Convergence: Some patients have a "convergence null" (nystagmus dampens when eyes converge). Base-Out (BO) prisms can be prescribed to force convergence at distance, thereby stabilizing vision.

2. Biofeedback

  • Auditory Biofeedback: A technique where eye movements are converted into auditory signals. The patient listens to the sound of their eye movements and uses conscious effort to reduce the "noise," thereby dampening the nystagmus.

3. Medical and Surgical Management

  • Botulinum Toxin: Can be injected into the extraocular muscles to temporarily paralyze them and dampen nystagmus, though this may cause ptosis or diplopia.
  • Surgery (Anderson-Kestenbaum procedure): Performed to physically rotate the eyes into the null position, eliminating the need for an abnormal head posture.
  • Nystagmus Blockage Syndrome: A condition where a patient (usually an infant) subconsciously uses a large esotropia to dampen their nystagmus. Treatment often involves surgery for the esotropia.

III. Summary of Key Concepts for Week 12

  • Diagnostic Rule: If nystagmus appears only upon covering one eye, suspect Latent Nystagmus and look for a history of Infantile Esotropia.
  • Visual Acuity Testing: Never use a standard occluder (black patch) for a patient with latent nystagmus; use a high-plus lens (+6.00D) to blur the fellow eye instead.
  • Oscillopsia: Presence of oscillopsia strongly suggests acquired pathology; absence suggests congenital origin.
  • Therapy Goal: Treatment (prisms, surgery, CLs) does not eliminate nystagmus but aims to maximize foveation time (the brief period the eye is still) or correct head posture.
