Superposition & Coherence (When Waves Meet)

This week, we are looking at what happens when two or more light waves bump into each other. Since light travels as a wave, it doesn't just crash like two solid objects; instead, the waves blend together.

1. The Principle of Superposition (The "Adding" Rule)

  • What it is: The Superposition Principle is a fancy way of saying that when waves are combined, they simply add together.
  • How it works: Imagine two sets of ripples moving across a pond. When they cross paths, the new shape of the water is just the sum of the two ripples.
  • Constructive vs. Destructive Interference: When these waves combine, they "interfere" with each other.
  • If the peak of one wave perfectly lines up with the peak of another wave, they add together to make a "super-peak." This makes a brighter light (called a bright fringe).
  • If the peak of one wave lines up with the bottom (trough) of another wave, they subtract and cancel each other out, leaving a dark spot (a dark fringe).

2. Coherence (Marching to the Same Beat)

For waves to combine and create clear, noticeable patterns (like the bright and dark fringes mentioned above), they need to be "in sync" with each other. Scientists call this coherence. If waves are just bouncing around randomly, it is just a messy blur. If they are coherent, they are marching to the exact same beat.

There are two main types of coherence you need to know:

  • Temporal Coherence (Sync over Time):
  • What it means: This describes how well a wave stays in a steady, predictable rhythm over a period of time.
  • The Analogy: Think of a perfectly tuned metronome tapping a steady beat. A laser is a great example of high temporal coherence because it is usually a single, pure color (one wavelength) that wiggles at a perfectly constant rate. White light (like from a standard lightbulb or the sun) is made of many different colors and wavelengths mixed together, so it gets out of phase quickly and washes out the pattern.
  • Spatial Coherence (Sync across Space):
  • What it means: This describes how well different parts of a wave are lined up with each other as they travel through space.
  • The Analogy: Imagine a marching band where everyone is walking perfectly shoulder-to-shoulder in a straight line. If you take light from a single, tiny point source (like a pinhole), the waves spread out evenly and stay lined up with each other. If the light comes from a massive, wide source, the waves are coming from all different angles and don't line up neatly.
Reference: Principles of Physical Optics, Charles A. Bennett (2nd Edition)
