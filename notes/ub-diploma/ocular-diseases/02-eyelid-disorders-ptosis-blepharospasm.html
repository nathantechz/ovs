Eyelid Disorders: Ptosis and Blepharospasm

The four causes of ptosis, the five measurements that classify it, and how blepharospasm differs from a lid that simply cannot lift.

WCO competency mapping Category 3 — Ocular Diagnostic Services, with the measurement set sitting in Category 2. You are expected to measure and classify ptosis accurately and to recognise the neurogenic causes that need urgent referral.

1. What ptosis is, and its four causes

Ptosis is an abnormally low position of the upper lid; it may be congenital or acquired. Kanski 8e, p. 38

Kanski classifies it by mechanism:

  • Neurogenic ptosis — an innervational defect such as third nerve paresis or Horner syndrome.
  • Myogenic ptosis — a myopathy of the levator itself, or impaired transmission at the neuromuscular junction (neuromyopathic). Acquired myogenic ptosis occurs in myasthenia gravis, myotonic dystrophy and progressive external ophthalmoplegia.
  • Aponeurotic (involutional) ptosis — a defect in the levator aponeurosis. This is the common age-related form.
  • Mechanical ptosis — the gravitational effect of a mass, or scarring.

Kanski 8e, p. 38

2. The measurements

Ptosis is classified by numbers, not impressions. These are the five you record, and they are examined constantly.

Margin–reflex distance (MRD)

Measured to the corneal light reflex from a light held by the examiner on which the patient fixates. The normal measurement is 4–5 mm. Kanski 8e, p. 40

Palpebral fissure height

The distance between the upper and lower lid margins, measured in the pupillary plane. The upper lid margin normally rests about 2 mm below the upper limbus and the lower 1 mm above the lower limbus. The measurement is shorter in males (7–10 mm) than in females (8–12 mm). Kanski 8e, p. 40

Levator function (upper lid excursion)

This is a procedure — do it exactly:

  1. Place your thumb firmly against the patient's brow to negate the action of frontalis.
  2. Start with the eyes in downgaze.
  3. Ask the patient to look up as far as possible.
  4. Measure the amount of excursion with a rule.

Kanski 8e, p. 40, Fig. 1.51

Levator functionExcursion
Normal15 mm or more
Good12–14 mm
Fair5–11 mm
Poor4 mm or less

Kanski 8e, p. 40

If you forget the thumb, the number is wrongFrontalis will lift the brow and inflate your excursion measurement, making poor levator function look fair. Since levator function decides which operation the patient gets, this is not a trivial error.

Upper lid crease

The vertical distance between the lid margin and the lid crease in downgaze — about 10 mm in females and 8 mm in males. Absence of the crease in congenital ptosis is evidence of poor levator function, whereas a high crease suggests an aponeurotic defect (usually involutional). Kanski 8e, p. 40

One measurement that gives you the diagnosisThe crease is the cheapest discriminator you have. High crease in an elderly patient with good levator function = aponeurotic ptosis. Absent crease in a child = poor levator function and congenital ptosis. You have narrowed the classification before touching anything else.

Why a diploma optometrist records theseYou will not perform the surgery, but your measurements decide which one is possible. A referral letter carrying MRD, fissure height, levator function and crease position is immediately actionable. One saying "droopy lid" is not.

4. Blepharospasm

Blepharospasm is involuntary forced closure of the lids by spasm of the orbicularis. It is a closing problem; ptosis is an opening problem. The distinction matters because the treatments are unrelated.

Botulinum toxin injection to the orbicularis muscle may help control the blepharospasm that often occurs in severe dry eye. Injected at the medial canthus it can also reduce tear drainage, presumably by limiting lid movement. Kanski 8e, p. 129

The link worth rememberingKanski places blepharospasm treatment inside the dry eye chapter. Severe ocular surface disease drives reflex spasm. If you meet blepharospasm, examine the ocular surface before assuming a primary neurological cause.

Also consider, and exclude:

  • Reflex spasm from any painful ocular surface condition — corneal foreign body, abrasion, severe dry eye
  • Hemifacial spasm — unilateral, involves the lower face as well, suggests a seventh nerve cause
  • Benign essential blepharospasm — bilateral, progressive, a focal dystonia, needs neurology input
