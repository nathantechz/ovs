Corneal Diseases (Part 1): Structure and Bacterial Keratitis

The cornea's dimensions, layers and nerve supply — then microbial keratitis, where contact lens wear is the single most important risk factor and the referral is same-day.

WCO competency mapping Category 3 — Ocular Diagnostic Services, with direct relevance to Category 1 contact lens practice. Recognising microbial keratitis and referring it urgently is a defining safety competency for anyone who fits lenses.

1. The cornea in numbers

The cornea is a complex structure which, as well as having a protective role, is responsible for about three-quarters of the optical power of the eye. The normal cornea is free of blood vessels; nutrients are supplied and metabolic products removed mainly via the aqueous humour posteriorly and the tears anteriorly. Kanski 8e, p. 168

The cornea is the most densely innervated tissue in the body, and conditions such as abrasions and bullous keratopathy are associated with marked pain, photophobia and reflex lacrimation. A subepithelial and a deeper stromal nerve plexus are both supplied by the first division of the trigeminal nerve. Kanski 8e, p. 168

The average corneal diameter is 11.5 mm vertically and 12 mm horizontally. It is 540 µm thick centrally on average, and thicker peripherally. Kanski 8e, p. 168

Three consequences you will use every weekThree-quarters of the eye's power is why a small corneal irregularity ruins vision and why topography matters. Avascularity is why the graft survives and why new vessels are always abnormal. Dense V1 innervation is why corneal problems hurt out of all proportion to their size — and why a painless corneal lesion should worry you, since it implies reduced sensation.

2. Bacterial keratitis — the risk factors

Contact lens wear, particularly if extended, is the most important risk factor. Corneal epithelial compromise secondary to hypoxia and minor trauma is thought to be important, as is bacterial adherence to the lens surface. Kanski 8e, p. 175

  • Wearers of soft lenses are at higher risk than those of rigid gas permeable and other types.
  • Infection is more likely if there is poor lens hygiene, but it can also occur even with apparently meticulous lens care, and with daily disposable lenses.
  • Trauma, including refractive surgery (particularly LASIK), has been linked to bacterial infection, including with atypical mycobacteria.

Kanski 8e, p. 175

Read the second bullet againKanski explicitly says infection occurs even with meticulous care and with daily disposables. Never reassure a symptomatic lens wearer on the grounds that their hygiene is good or their lenses are daily. The history does not exclude the diagnosis.

3. Recognising it, and acting

A contact lens wearer with a red, painful eye is microbial keratitis until proven otherwise. Look for:

  • A white or creamy stromal infiltrate, usually with an overlying epithelial defect that stains with fluorescein
  • Marked pain, photophobia and reflex lacrimation — expected, given V1 innervation
  • Anterior chamber activity, possibly hypopyon
  • Conjunctival injection, worst circumcorneally

What to do, in order:

  1. Remove the lens and do not replace it.
  2. Do not patch the eye.
  3. Do not start a topical steroid.
  4. Retain the lens, case and solutions — they may be cultured.
  5. Refer the same day for scraping and intensive topical antibiotics.
The distinction that decides urgencyA sterile peripheral infiltrate in a lens wearer is typically small, peripheral, multiple, with an intact or minimally stained epithelium and little pain. Microbial keratitis is typically single, larger, more central, with a definite epithelial defect, significant pain and anterior chamber activity. If you cannot confidently place it in the first group, treat it as the second.

4. Other management options Kanski lists

For persistent epithelial defects and threatened perforation, the options in the surrounding text are worth knowing so your referral letter is informed:

  • Temporary or permanent lateral tarsorrhaphy or medial canthoplasty, and occasionally central tarsorrhaphy.
  • Conjunctival (Gundersen) flap — protects and tends to heal a corneal epithelial defect; particularly suitable for chronic unilateral disease with poor visual prognosis. Buccal mucous membrane is an alternative.
  • Amniotic membrane patch grafting for persistent unresponsive epithelial defects.
  • Tissue adhesive (cyanoacrylate glue) to seal small perforations.

Kanski 8e, p. 175
