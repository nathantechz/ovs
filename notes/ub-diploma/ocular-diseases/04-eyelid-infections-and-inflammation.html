Eyelid Infections and Inflammation

Chronic blepharitis in its anterior and posterior forms — why the symptoms never match the signs, the table that separates the three types, and the lid hygiene that actually works.

WCO competency mapping Category 3 — Ocular Diagnostic Services. Blepharitis is the commonest lid condition you will meet and the one most often managed entirely within diploma scope through lid hygiene and ocular lubrication.

1. Why blepharitis is difficult

Chronic blepharitis (chronic marginal blepharitis) is a very common cause of ocular discomfort and irritation. Kanski is unusually frank about why it frustrates clinicians: the poor correlation between symptoms and signs, the uncertain aetiology and mechanisms of the disease process all combine to make management difficult. Kanski 8e, p. 34

Set the patient's expectations on day oneBecause symptoms and signs correlate poorly, a patient can look better and feel no different. Tell them at the first visit that this is a condition to be controlled rather than cured, and that hygiene is lifelong. Patients who are not told this stop after two weeks and conclude the treatment failed.

2. Anterior and posterior — the division

Blepharitis may be subdivided into anterior and posterior, although there is considerable overlap and both types are often present (mixed blepharitis). Kanski 8e, p. 34

Anterior blepharitis

  • Affects the area surrounding the bases of the eyelashes; may be staphylococcal or seborrhoeic.
  • Regarded as related more to chronic infective elements and hence more amenable to treatment and remission than the posterior form.
  • In staphylococcal blepharitis an aetiological factor may be an abnormal cell-mediated response to components of the cell wall of S. aureus, which may also be responsible for the red eyes and peripheral corneal infiltrates seen in some patients. It is more common and more marked in patients with atopic dermatitis.
  • Seborrhoeic blepharitis is strongly associated with generalised seborrhoeic dermatitis involving the scalp, nasolabial folds, skin behind the ears and the sternum.

Kanski 8e, p. 34

Posterior blepharitis

  • Caused by meibomian gland dysfunction and alterations in meibomian gland secretions.
  • Bacterial lipases may result in the formation of free fatty acids. This increases the melting point of the meibum, preventing its expression from the glands, contributing to ocular surface irritation and possibly enabling growth of S. aureus.
  • Loss of the tear film phospholipids that act as surfactants results in increased tear evaporation and osmolarity, and an unstable tear film.
  • Commonly a more persistent and chronic inflammatory condition than anterior blepharitis; associated with acne rosacea.

Kanski 8e, p. 34

Why warm compresses work — the mechanism, not the ritualFree fatty acids raise the melting point of meibum so it will not flow. Heat lowers the viscosity back to where expression is possible. That is the whole rationale, and it tells you the compress must be genuinely warm and held long enough to heat the gland — not a brief wipe with a tepid flannel.

3. The Demodex question

A reaction to the hair follicle and sebaceous gland-dwelling mite Demodex may play a causative role in some patients — D. folliculorum longus in anterior blepharitis and D. folliculorum brevis in posterior blepharitis. Kanski is careful: the mite can be found normally in a majority of older patients, most of whom do not develop symptomatic blepharitis. Overpopulation or hypersensitivity may lead to symptoms. Kanski 8e, p. 34

Finding the mite is not finding the causeMost older patients carry Demodex without symptoms. Cylindrical collarettes at the lash base are the suggestive sign; the mere presence of mites is not.

5. Symptoms and signs

Involvement is usually bilateral and symmetrical. Symptoms are caused by disruption of normal ocular surface function and reduction in tear stability, and are similar in all forms, though stinging may be more common in posterior disease. Burning, grittiness, mild photophobia, and crusting and redness of the lid margins with remissions and exacerbations are characteristic. Kanski 8e, p. 34

The timing clueSymptoms are usually worse in the mornings, although in patients with associated dry eye they may increase during the day. A patient who is worst on waking points to blepharitis; one who deteriorates through the day points to a dry eye component. Kanski 8e, pp. 34–35

Signs — staphylococcal

  • Hard scales and crusting mainly located around the bases of the lashes; collarettes are cylindrical collections around lash bases.
  • Mild papillary conjunctivitis and chronic conjunctival hyperaemia are common.
  • Long-standing cases may develop scarring and notching (tylosis) of the lid margin, madarosis, trichiasis and poliosis.
  • Associated tear film instability and dry eye.

Kanski 8e, p. 35

Signs — posterior (meibomian gland disease)

  • Excessive and abnormal meibomian gland secretion, manifesting as capping of meibomian gland orifices with oil globules.
  • Pouting, recession, or plugging of meibomian gland orifices.
  • Hyperaemia and telangiectasis of the posterior lid margin.
  • Pressure on the lid margin results in expression of meibomian fluid that may be turbid or toothpaste-like; in severe cases secretions become so inspissated that expression is impossible.

Kanski 8e, p. 35

Express the glands — it is a two-second testGentle pressure on the lid margin with a cotton bud or your fingertip against the globe tells you the grade immediately: clear oil is normal, turbid or toothpaste-like is MGD, nothing at all is severe inspissation. Record what came out, not just that the margins looked red.

6. Related lid infections

Childhood blepharokeratoconjunctivitis

A poorly defined condition that tends to be more severe in Asian and Middle Eastern populations — directly relevant to practice in Saudi Arabia. Presentation is usually at about 6 years of age with recurrent episodes of anterior or posterior blepharitis, sometimes with recurrent styes or chalazia. Constant eye rubbing and photophobia may lead to misdiagnosis as allergic eye disease. Kanski 8e, p. 38

Findings and management:

  • Conjunctival changes: diffuse hyperaemia, bulbar phlyctens, follicular or papillary hyperplasia.
  • Corneal changes: superficial punctate keratopathy, marginal keratitis, peripheral vascularisation, axial subepithelial haze.
  • Treatment is with lid hygiene and topical antibiotic ointment at bedtime. Topical low-dose steroids (prednisolone 0.1% or fluorometholone 0.1%) and erythromycin syrup 125 mg daily for 4–6 weeks may also be used.

Kanski 8e, p. 38

A child with itchy eyes in this regionKanski names this as more severe in Middle Eastern populations and warns it is misdiagnosed as allergy. A six-year-old rubbing their eyes with photophobia deserves a lid margin examination before an antihistamine.

Tick infestation of the eyelid

Ticks can attach to the eyelid and should be removed at the earliest opportunity to minimise the risk of a tick-borne zoonosis such as Lyme disease. It is critical that the tick is detached as close to its skin attachment as possible in order to remove its head and mouthparts. Lyme disease transmission is thought to require attachment of the tick for at least 36 hours. Kanski 8e, p. 38
