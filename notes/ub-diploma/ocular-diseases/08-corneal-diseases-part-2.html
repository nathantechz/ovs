Corneal Diseases (Part 2): Viral Keratitis and Ectasia

Herpes simplex and herpes zoster at the cornea, the stain that gives the diagnosis away, the drug that must never be given blind, and keratoconus — the ectasia a diploma optometrist is most likely to detect first.

2. Herpes simplex keratitis

The epithelial (dendritic) form is the one you must recognise without hesitation:

  • A branching, linear epithelial lesion with terminal bulbs and swollen epithelial cells at the margins.
  • The ulcer bed stains with fluorescein; the swollen cells at the margins stain with rose Bengal. The two stains show different things — use both if you can.
  • Corneal sensation is reduced. Test it before instilling anything, because anaesthetic destroys the test.
The single most important rule in this topicNever start a topical steroid in an undiagnosed red eye with an epithelial defect. Steroid applied to a dendritic ulcer converts it into a geographic ulcer and can cost the eye. If you are not certain what you are looking at, you refer — you do not treat.
Test sensation first, alwaysCorneal sensation is the one test that anaesthetic, fluorescein and even prolonged lid manipulation will degrade. A wisp of cotton wool to the cornea before anything else takes ten seconds and can point straight at a herpetic or neurotrophic cause.

For the stromal and severe forms Kanski notes that IOP should be monitored, that perforation — actual or impending — is managed as for bacterial keratitis, and that therapeutic keratoplasty is considered when medical therapy is ineffective or following perforation. Kanski 8e, p. 183

3. Herpes zoster ophthalmicus

  • Reactivation of varicella zoster in the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve (V1) — the same division that supplies the cornea.
  • Hutchinson sign — vesicles on the side or tip of the nose, indicating nasociliary branch involvement and a higher likelihood of ocular disease.
  • The rash respects the midline, which distinguishes it from most other facial rashes.
  • Ocular complications are wide-ranging: keratitis, uveitis, raised IOP, scleritis and, later, post-herpetic neuralgia.

Herpes zoster ophthalmicus, Kanski 8e from p. 189

Check the noseHutchinson sign is free information. A patient with a V1 rash and vesicles on the nose tip needs ocular examination urgently, because the nasociliary nerve supplies both the nose tip and the globe.

4. Corneal ectasia — keratoconus

Kanski places corneal ectasias at p. 213. Keratoconus matters disproportionately to you because the earliest signs appear in a refraction, not in a clinic:

  • Progressive irregular astigmatism, often with frequent spectacle changes and poor best-corrected acuity.
  • Scissoring reflex on retinoscopy — frequently the first objective sign, and one you will see before anyone else does.
  • Munson sign — V-shaped indentation of the lower lid in downgaze, a late sign.
  • Vogt striae — fine vertical stromal lines that disappear on digital pressure.
  • Fleischer ring — iron deposition at the base of the cone, best seen with a cobalt blue filter.
  • Acute hydrops — sudden painful vision loss with corneal oedema from a break in Descemet membrane.
Why the scissoring reflex is your signA young patient whose astigmatism keeps changing and whose retinoscopy reflex scissors is keratoconus until topography says otherwise. Detecting it early matters more now than it used to, because cross-linking can halt progression — see the next section.

5. What is happening now

Kanski 8e is from 2016, and keratoconus management is the clearest example in this course of a textbook being overtaken. The signs above are unchanged; what has changed is what happens after the diagnosis.

  • Corneal cross-linking (CXL) has moved keratoconus from a condition that was optically managed while it progressed, to one where progression itself can be arrested. This makes early detection — your scissoring reflex — genuinely sight-saving rather than merely diagnostic.
  • Scleral lenses have substantially expanded what can be fitted before keratoplasty is considered.
  • Topography and tomography now detect subclinical disease well before slit lamp signs appear, which is why a suspicious refraction warrants imaging rather than watchful waiting.
The practical consequenceIn 2016 a young keratoconic was refracted and reviewed. Today they are imaged, and if progression is documented they are referred for cross-linking. Your threshold for topography in a young patient with changing astigmatism should be low.
