Retinal Diseases: PVD, Breaks and Detachment

Posterior vitreous detachment — what it is, how common it is, and why it matters — then retinal breaks and the detachment that follows, with the symptoms that make this an emergency referral.

WCO competency mapping Category 3 — Ocular Diagnostic Services. Recognising the symptoms of PVD and retinal detachment, and referring with correct urgency, is among the highest-stakes decisions a diploma optometrist makes.

1. Posterior vitreous detachment

Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) refers to separation of the cortical vitreous, along with the delineating posterior hyaloid membrane (PHM), from the neurosensory retina posterior to the vitreous base. Kanski 8e, p. 694

The mechanism has two named stages:

  • Synchysis — vitreous gel liquefaction with age, forming fluid-filled cavities.
  • Syneresis — subsequent condensation, with access to the preretinal space allowed by a dehiscence in the cortical gel and/or the posterior hyaloid membrane.

Kanski 8e, p. 694

The prevalence of PVD increases with age, and in individuals in their 80s is likely to be at least 60%. It is typically spontaneous, but can be induced by events such as cataract surgery, trauma, uveitis and panretinal photocoagulation. The time taken for a PVD to complete after initiation is believed to be variable, but probably occurs in stages over the course of months in many patients. Kanski 8e, p. 694

"In stages over months" is the safety messageA PVD that looks uncomplicated today can still tear the retina next week, because the separation is not finished. That is precisely why every patient you send home after a PVD assessment must be told to return immediately if symptoms change.

2. The symptoms that matter

SymptomWhat it suggestsUrgency
New floatersPVD; possible vitreous haemorrhage if sudden showerSame-day assessment
Flashes (photopsia)Vitreoretinal traction — the retina is being pulledSame-day assessment
A shower of dark spotsVitreous haemorrhage, often from a torn vessel at a breakUrgent
A curtain or shadow in the fieldRetinal detachmentEmergency
Central vision lossMacula-off detachmentEmergency
The macula-on / macula-off distinctionIf central vision is still good, the macula is probably still attached — and surgery to reattach it before the macula detaches gives a far better visual outcome. That is why a detachment with intact central vision is more urgent, not less. Say so explicitly when you refer.

3. Peripheral retinal findings

Kanski's peripheral retina section describes appearances you will meet on dilated examination and must be able to tell apart from pathology:

  • White with pressure and white without pressure — greyish-white appearance of the peripheral retina, seen with and without scleral indentation respectively. Note that condensed vitreous gel can be strongly attached to an area of white without pressure.
  • Lattice degeneration — the most important predisposing lesion to retinal break formation.
  • In myopic eyes, retinal holes developing in atrophic retina may occasionally lead to retinal detachment. Because of lack of contrast, small holes may be very difficult to visualise.

Kanski 8e, p. 694, Fig. 16.19

Myopes deserve more careKanski notes small holes in atrophic myopic retina are hard to see for lack of contrast. Combine that with the higher baseline risk in myopia and the message is simple: dilate myopic patients, examine the periphery properly, and take their symptoms seriously.

4. Types of retinal detachment

ExudativeFluid accumulates beneath the retina without a break — inflammation, tumour, vascular diseasep. 712

Chapter 16 contents, Kanski 8e

Rhegma- means a breakThe word does the work. Rhegmatogenous = there is a hole. If there is no hole, the fluid arrived another way — pulled (tractional) or leaked (exudative) — and the cause, and the treatment, are different.

5. What is happening now

  • Widefield and ultra-widefield imaging now documents peripheral pathology that previously depended entirely on indirect ophthalmoscopy with indentation, making findings shareable with the referral unit.
  • OCT resolves vitreoretinal interface anatomy directly, so partial PVD, vitreomacular traction and macular involvement can be confirmed rather than inferred.
  • Neither replaces the clinical skills above. A curtain in the field is still an emergency referral whether or not you have imaging.
The thing technology has not changedThe decisive act in retinal detachment is still recognising the symptom and moving quickly. No instrument improves on a clinician who asks about flashes, floaters and field loss, and who acts the same day.
