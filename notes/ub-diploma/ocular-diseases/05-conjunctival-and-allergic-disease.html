Conjunctival Diseases and Allergic Conjunctivitis

Conjunctival anatomy and its immune tissue, the symptom that identifies allergy, and the papillae-versus-follicles distinction that sorts most red eyes at the slit lamp.

WCO competency mapping Category 3 — Ocular Diagnostic Services. Differentiating the causes of a red eye and recognising which need referral is core diploma work; topical anti-allergy therapy depends on national scope.

1. Anatomy — three parts, one membrane

The conjunctiva is a transparent mucous membrane that lines the inner surface of the eyelids and the anterior surface of the globe, terminating at the corneoscleral limbus. It is richly vascular, supplied by the anterior ciliary and palpebral arteries. There is a dense lymphatic network draining to the preauricular and submandibular nodes. It has a key protective role, mediating both passive and active immunity. Kanski 8e, p. 132

Anatomically it divides into:

  • Palpebral conjunctiva — starts at the mucocutaneous junction of the lid margins and is firmly attached to the posterior tarsal plates. The tarsal blood vessels are vertically orientated.
  • Forniceal conjunctiva — loose and redundant.
  • Bulbar conjunctiva — covers the anterior sclera.

Kanski 8e, p. 132

Why you palpate the preauricular nodeConjunctival lymphatics drain to the preauricular and submandibular nodes. A palpable, tender preauricular node in a red eye is a strong pointer to viral conjunctivitis. It costs five seconds and is one of the most discriminating signs available to you.

2. CALT — the conjunctiva's immune tissue

Conjunctiva-associated lymphoid tissue (CALT) is critical in the initiation and regulation of ocular surface immune responses. It consists of lymphocytes within the epithelial layers, lymphatics and associated blood vessels, with a stromal component of lymphocytes and plasma cells, including follicular aggregates. Kanski 8e, p. 132

This explains folliclesFollicles are visible CALT. When the conjunctiva mounts a lymphoid response — viral or chlamydial infection — those aggregates enlarge and become the pale, avascular-centred bumps you see in the fornix. Knowing what they are is what makes the papillae/follicle distinction memorable rather than arbitrary.

3. Symptoms — and the one that matters

Non-specific symptoms include lacrimation, grittiness, stinging and burning. Two findings carry far more weight: Kanski 8e, p. 132

  • Itching is the hallmark of allergic disease, although it may also occur to a lesser extent in blepharitis and dry eye.
  • Significant pain, photophobia or a marked foreign body sensation suggest corneal involvement — which moves the problem out of the conjunctiva chapter entirely.

Kanski 8e, p. 132

Ask one question first"Does it itch, or does it hurt?" Itch sends you toward allergy. Pain with photophobia sends you to the cornea and raises the urgency. Almost no other single question separates red eyes so efficiently.

4. Papillae versus follicles

PapillaeFollicles
Vascular patternVessel at the centre, branching outwardVessels around the periphery, pale avascular centre
Underlying tissueVascular/inflammatory hyperplasiaLymphoid aggregates (CALT)
Typical causesAllergic disease, bacterial infection, chronic irritation, contact lens wearViral infection, chlamydial infection, drug toxicity
Best seenUpper tarsal conjunctiva (evert the lid)Lower fornix and tarsal conjunctiva

You must evert the lidGiant papillae in vernal disease and contact lens papillary conjunctivitis live on the upper tarsal conjunctiva. If you do not evert, you do not see them, and you will attribute the patient's lens intolerance to something else.

5. Allergic conjunctivitis in practice

The allergic group is united by itch and by papillae, and separated by severity and corneal involvement:

  • Seasonal and perennial allergic conjunctivitis — itch, watering, mild papillae. No corneal threat.
  • Vernal keratoconjunctivitis — young patients, often male, giant papillae on the upper tarsus, limbal Horner–Trantas dots, and a real risk of shield ulcer. Corneal involvement makes this sight-threatening.
  • Atopic keratoconjunctivitis — older patients with atopic dermatitis; chronic, scarring, corneal involvement common.
  • Contact lens papillary conjunctivitis — papillae from mechanical and immunological response to the lens or its deposits. Managed by changing lens, modality or care system.
The one that is not benignSeasonal allergy is uncomfortable. Vernal disease with corneal involvement is sight-threatening. A young patient with severe itch, giant papillae and any corneal staining is a referral, not a bottle of antihistamine.
