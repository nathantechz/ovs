Complications of Soft Lenses (continued)

The inflammatory, mechanical and infective complications — and the distinction between a sterile infiltrate and microbial keratitis, which is the most consequential judgement in contact lens practice.

WCO competency mapping Category 3 — Ocular Diagnostic Services. Same-day recognition and referral of microbial keratitis is a defining safety competency.

1. Papillary conjunctivitis

  • Papillae on the upper tarsal conjunctiva — the reason lid eversion is not optional in a symptomatic wearer.
  • Caused by a combined mechanical and immunological response to the lens edge, surface deposits or care solutions.
  • Symptoms: itch, mucus, increasing lens awareness, shortening wearing time and lens mobility on blink.
  • Managed by changing lens, modality or care system — often moving to daily disposable removes the deposit and the solution together.
Evert, or miss itKanski places giant papillae on the upper tarsal conjunctiva. A wearer complaining of reducing wearing time whose lids you have not everted has not been examined.

2. Solution-related reactions

"The use of strong disinfectants (many of which are also used in lower concentrations as preservatives) in chemical-based disinfection systems can cause problems for the patient." IACLE M5, p. 12

  • Diffuse punctate staining, worst where the solution pools.
  • Stinging on insertion, redness settling through the day.
  • A peroxide system inserted without neutralisation causes immediate, severe pain and a chemical injury — a genuine emergency.
Change one thing at a timeWhen lens, modality and solution are all changed at once and the patient improves, you have learned nothing about the cause. Change the solution first — it is the cheapest variable and often the culprit.

3. Mechanical complications

  • Superior epithelial arcuate lesion (SEAL) — an arc of epithelial disruption in the superior cornea, from mechanical interaction between a stiffer lens and the upper lid. More associated with higher-modulus materials.
  • Tight lens syndrome — a dehydrated or steep lens binds, giving pain, injection and an indentation ring after removal.
  • Lens dehydration staining — punctate staining in the pattern of the lens, worse in dry environments and toward the end of the day.

4. The judgement that matters

Contact lens wear, particularly if extended, is the most important risk factor for bacterial keratitis. Wearers of soft lenses are at higher risk than those of rigid gas permeable and other types. Infection is more likely with poor lens hygiene, but it can also occur even with apparently meticulous lens care, and with daily disposable lenses. Kanski 8e, p. 175

The rule when you are unsureIf you cannot place a lesion confidently in the left-hand column, treat it as the right-hand one. The cost of referring a sterile infiltrate is an unnecessary appointment. The cost of observing a microbial keratitis is a cornea.

On suspicion, in order:

  1. Remove the lens and do not replace it.
  2. Do not patch the eye.
  3. Do not start a topical steroid.
  4. Retain the lens, case and solutions — they may be cultured.
  5. Refer the same day for scraping and intensive topical antibiotics.
AcanthamoebaKanski gives protozoan keratitis its own section p. 197. Suspect it in a lens wearer with pain markedly out of proportion to the signs, particularly with any history of water exposure — tap water rinsing, swimming or showering in lenses. It is the reason the tap-water rule is absolute.

Check yourself

  • Where do the papillae of contact lens papillary conjunctivitis form, and what does that mean for your examination?
  • Give three signs of a solution-related reaction.
  • What is a SEAL and which material property is it associated with?
  • List four features that distinguish microbial keratitis from a sterile infiltrate.
  • Does meticulous hygiene exclude microbial keratitis?
  • State the five immediate actions on suspicion of microbial keratitis.
  • Which organism should you suspect when pain is out of proportion to the signs?
