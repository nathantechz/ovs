Optical Properties of Soft Lenses

Why a soft lens has no tear lens, what flexure does to the correction, how vertex distance changes the power you order, and the magnification difference that makes lenses valuable in anisometropia.

WCO competency mapping Category 1 — Optical Technology Services. Power determination, over-refraction and management of anisometropia.

1. No tear lens — the defining simplification

When a flexible lens is placed on the cornea, the 'tear lens' under the contact lens is very thin. It has no dioptric power due to the conformity of the lens to the shape of the cornea. IACLE M2, p. 135

What this buys youBecause there is no tear lens to compensate for, soft lens power is the ocular refraction corrected for vertex distance — nothing more. Changing the BOZR of a soft lens changes the fit, not the power. That is the opposite of a rigid lens, and it is the single most useful contrast in this course.

2. Flexure — the complication that returns

The same conformity that removes the tear lens creates a different problem: the lens takes on the shape of what it drapes over. IACLE notes that the tear lens under a rigid lens depends on the fitting relationship and, to a lesser extent, the material's rigidity — rigidity being exactly what a soft lens lacks. IACLE M2, p. 135

The practical consequences:

  • Corneal astigmatism is not masked. A soft sphere wraps onto a toric cornea and reproduces its astigmatism, so significant cylinder needs a toric lens rather than a spherical one.
  • Thickness resists flexure. A thicker lens flexes less — but thickness also lowers Dk/t, so the two requirements pull against each other.
  • Toric lenses must be stabilised, since a rotating cylinder is worse than none.

3. Vertex distance

A spectacle lens sits roughly 12–14 mm in front of the cornea; a contact lens sits on it. The effective power differs, and the difference grows with the power of the correction. IACLE M2, p. 104

Fc = Fs / (1 − d · Fs)Fs = spectacle power (D) · Fc = contact lens power (D) · d = vertex distance in metres
Spectacle RxDirection of changeRoughly
Low (under about ±4 D)NegligibleNo change
High minusNeeds less minuse.g. −10.00 → about −9.00
High plusNeeds more pluse.g. +10.00 → about +11.00
The threshold to rememberCompensate above about ±4.00 D. Below that the change is inside your measurement error; above it, failing to compensate is the reason a high myope complains their new lenses are over-minused.

4. Magnification and anisometropia

IACLE devotes a section to Magnification and specifically to Spectacle and Contact Lens magnification. IACLE M2, p. 104

  • A minus spectacle lens minifies the retinal image; a plus spectacle lens magnifies it. The effect grows with power and with vertex distance.
  • A contact lens sits at the cornea, so the magnification difference between the two eyes is far smaller.
  • In anisometropia, spectacles therefore produce unequal retinal image sizes (aniseikonia), which may prevent comfortable fusion. Contact lenses largely remove this.
The strongest optical argument for contact lensesFor a patient with significant anisometropia, contact lenses are not a cosmetic preference — they are the correction that makes binocular vision possible. Evans makes the related point that anisometropic amblyopes may not perceive an immediate benefit on insertion and so are more likely to drop out Evans, p. 82: warn them, or you will lose them in the first fortnight.

5. Over-refraction

IACLE provides a dedicated practical, Practical 2.3 Contact Lens Over-Refraction. IACLE M2, p. 104

For a soft lens:

  1. Insert a trial lens of known BVP and allow it to settle — a lens refracted immediately on insertion gives a misleading result.
  2. Check the fit first: movement, centration, coverage. An over-refraction through a poorly fitting lens measures the misfit, not the eye.
  3. Over-refract. Ordered BVP = trial BVP + over-refraction.
  4. If the over-refraction contains unexpected cylinder, suspect flexure over a toric cornea before assuming the spectacle refraction was wrong.
