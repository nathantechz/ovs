Complications of Soft Lenses

The hypoxic and metabolic consequences of covering a cornea that has no blood supply — oedema, microcysts, neovascularisation — and how to grade what you see.

WCO competency mapping Category 3 — Ocular Diagnostic Services. Detection, grading and the decision to modify, suspend or refer.

1. Start from the physiology

The cornea is free of blood vessels; nutrients are supplied and metabolic products removed mainly via the aqueous humour posteriorly and the tears anteriorly. Kanski 8e, p. 168

A contact lens sits on the anterior supply routeEvery soft lens complication in this topic follows from that single sentence. Cover the anterior surface and you interfere with the tear-borne supply of oxygen and the removal of metabolic products. The lens does not injure the cornea; it starves it.

IACLE frames fitting as meeting a demand: select a lens which allows a level of oxygen above, and preferably well above, the 'average' minimum required, noting general agreement on the levels needed for safe daily and overnight wear. IACLE M6, p. 5

2. Corneal oedema

The first and most direct consequence. Graded by what appears as it worsens:

  • Subtle — detectable only by pachymetry; no slit lamp sign.
  • Striae — fine vertical greyish-white lines in the posterior stroma. The first visible sign, and the one to hunt for.
  • Folds — in Descemet membrane, indicating greater swelling.
  • Gross oedema — epithelial haze and reduced vision.
The symptoms are still Müller'sProgressive veiling of objects and coloured haloes around lights IACLE M2, p. 12. A patient reporting haloes at the end of the day is describing oedema, whatever else they say. Ask when in the day it happens — end-of-day haloes point at the lens, on-waking haloes at overnight wear.

3. Epithelial microcysts

  • Small, discrete, irregular inclusions in the epithelium, seen best on marginal retroillumination, showing reversed illumination — the optical signature that distinguishes them from vacuoles.
  • A marker of chronic metabolic disturbance, not an acute event. They take weeks of altered epithelial turnover to appear.
  • Counterintuitive behaviour on treatment: when the hypoxic stimulus is removed, microcysts transiently increase before resolving, as the backlog of affected cells moves to the surface.
A rebound is success, not failureYou move a patient into a higher-Dk lens and the microcyst count goes up at the next visit. That is the expected course. Record it, explain it, and review again rather than reversing a correct decision.

4. Neovascularisation

  • New vessels growing in from the limbus into a tissue that is normally avascular.
  • Driven by chronic hypoxia and by inflammation.
  • Vessels do not regress fully once established — they may empty and become ghost vessels, but the structural change persists.
  • Significant encroachment on the visual axis threatens vision and prejudices any future corneal graft.
Measure it, do not just note itRecord the extent in millimetres of encroachment from the limbus and the clock hours involved. "Some neovascularisation" at two consecutive visits tells you nothing; "1.2 mm at 3 o'clock, previously 0.8 mm" tells you to change the lens today.

5. Reduced corneal sensitivity

The cornea is the most densely innervated tissue in the body, supplied by the first division of the trigeminal nerve. Kanski 8e, p. 168

Why this is the most dangerous complicationChronic lens wear reduces corneal sensitivity. A cornea that cannot feel pain does not warn its owner about an ulcer. The long-term wearer who says "it doesn't really hurt" may be the one in most trouble — never let the absence of pain reassure you in a lens wearer with a corneal lesion.

Check yourself

  • Why does covering the cornea with a lens cause metabolic problems?
  • Name the visible signs of corneal oedema in order of increasing severity.
  • How are microcysts best viewed, and what optical sign identifies them?
  • Why do microcysts increase after you improve a patient's oxygen supply?
  • Do corneal new vessels regress completely when the cause is removed?
  • How should neovascularisation be recorded so that progression is detectable?
  • Why is reduced corneal sensitivity dangerous rather than convenient?
