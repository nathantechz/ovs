Soft Contact Lenses: Materials, Manufacturing, Design, Use

PHEMA and what followed, how hydration changes a finished lens, the design factors that alter on-eye behaviour — and an honest account of where this First Edition textbook stops.

WCO competency mapping Category 1 — Optical Technology Services. Material and modality selection, and patient instruction, are performed directly.

1. The founding material

Wichterle and Lim's poly-hydroxyethyl-methacrylate (PHEMA) is a water-absorbing polymer at 38.6% water, permeable to nutrients and metabolites. IACLE M2, p. 21

Water was the oxygen strategyIn a conventional hydrogel, oxygen crosses the lens dissolved in its water. Raise the water content and you raise Dk — but you also make the lens more fragile, more prone to dehydration and more deposit-attracting. Every conventional soft lens is a compromise along that one axis.

2. Hydration changes the lens you made

A soft lens is manufactured dry and worn wet, and the two states differ. IACLE's quality control notes on the wet state are practical and specific: IACLE M2, p. 67

  • BOZR is critical. It influences the lens fit and the optics of the tear lens. The BOZR will be approximately 0.03 mm flatter after hydration.
  • Image quality is reassessed to determine the optical quality of the product; vision quality depends on the prescription being accurate and the quality of the optics.
  • Workmanship is reassessed to confirm that hydration has not revealed previously undetected defects. Defects may include edge chips and surface scratches.

IACLE M2, p. 67

Verify wet, alwaysA lens measured dry is not the lens the patient wears — the BOZR alone shifts about 0.03 mm flatter on hydration. Any verification that matters is done in the hydrated state.

3. Design factors

IACLE lists the soft lens design factors, each of which can affect 'on-eye' performance:

  • Geometric centre thickness (tc) — sets Dk/t, handling and the degree to which the lens drapes.
  • Total diameter (TD, ØT) — governs corneal coverage and limbal relationship.
  • Back optic zone radius (BOZR, r0) — with diameter, determines sagittal height and therefore tightness.
  • Back surface design.

IACLE M2, p. 162

Judge a soft fit by sagittal height, not BOZR aloneBOZR and diameter act together: a lens can be made tighter by steepening the radius or by increasing the diameter. When a soft lens is too tight or too loose, either parameter is a lever.

4. What is happening now

Where the textbook stopsThe IACLE modules are First Edition and predate silicone hydrogels. Searching the whole library for the term returns a single passing line, in Evans on paediatric fitting Evans, p. 82. Everything in this section is therefore flagged as current practice beyond the cited texts, and none of it should be attributed to IACLE.

The four developments that changed soft lens practice after this textbook:

  • Silicone hydrogels. Oxygen travels through the silicone phase rather than the water, breaking the water–Dk link entirely. Dk/t rose several-fold, which is why overnight wear became physiologically defensible. The trade-offs moved to modulus (stiffer material, mechanical complications) and surface wettability.
  • Daily disposables. A lens used once removes the storage case, the solution and the compliance problem — the three places infection risk concentrates. Note Kanski's caution that infection can still occur with daily disposables Kanski 8e, p. 175.
  • Myopia control designs. Dual-focus and peripheral-defocus soft lenses give contact lenses a therapeutic role in slowing axial elongation in children.
  • Modern scleral lenses. Gas-permeable sclerals for irregular corneas and ocular surface disease — a return to the lens shape Fick and Kalt used in 1888, now in materials that let the cornea breathe.
The exam answer, and the clinic answerIf asked how water content relates to oxygen performance, the textbook answer is that higher water carries more oxygen — true for conventional hydrogels. Add that this no longer holds for silicone hydrogels, where a lower water material can transmit far more oxygen. Knowing which rule applies to which material is the competency.

Check yourself

  • Name the founding soft lens material, its water content and who discovered it.
  • In a conventional hydrogel, how does oxygen reach the cornea?
  • By how much does BOZR change on hydration, and in which direction?
  • Why must soft lens verification be done in the hydrated state?
  • List the soft lens design factors and say which two together determine tightness.
  • Explain why the water-content rule does not hold for silicone hydrogels.
  • Does a daily disposable modality eliminate the risk of microbial keratitis?
