Complications of Rigid Lenses

Hypoxia and its signs, mechanical effects of a lens that does not drape, and the one complication that must never be managed in the chair.

WCO competency mapping Category 3 — Ocular Diagnostic Services. Recognising and grading complications, and referring microbial keratitis the same day.

1. Oxygen is the underlying variable

IACLE frames the whole subject around supply and demand: a lens should be selected which allows a level of oxygen above, and preferably well above, the 'average' minimum required. Considerable research has established general agreement on the oxygen levels required for safe daily and overnight wear, and the module notes that many lenses historically marketed were incapable of meeting those levels, especially for overnight extended wear. IACLE M6, p. 5

The historical point that still mattersThe textbook admits the industry sold lenses that could not meet the published oxygen requirements. That is the context for every hypoxic complication described below — and the reason material Dk improved so sharply afterwards.

2. Signs of hypoxia

August Müller described the symptoms in 1889 — veiling of vision and coloured haloes around lights IACLE M2, p. 12. The signs you look for:

  • Corneal oedema — the direct consequence; striae and folds appear as it increases.
  • Epithelial microcysts — a marker of chronic metabolic stress rather than acute insult, appearing weeks into the problem and, importantly, increasing transiently when the cause is removed.
  • Neovascularisation — vessels growing into a tissue that Kanski reminds us is normally free of blood vessels Kanski 8e, p. 168.
  • Reduced corneal sensitivity with chronic wear.
Any new corneal vessel is abnormalThe cornea is avascular by design. A vessel crossing the limbus in a lens wearer is the eye responding to a problem you have not yet named. Find it before the vessel gets further.

3. Mechanical complications

A rigid lens does not conform to the cornea, which creates problems a soft lens does not:

  • 3 and 9 o'clock staining — peripheral desiccation where the lid fails to resurface the cornea beside the lens edge.
  • Corneal abrasion from a foreign body trapped beneath the lens — rigid lenses trap debris that a soft lens would not.
  • Lens binding / adherence, particularly after overnight wear.
  • Corneal warpage and spectacle blur — the cornea moulds to the lens, so spectacle vision is poor immediately after removal.
  • Ptosis with long-term rigid wear.
Spectacle blur is a fitting signalA patient who cannot see with their glasses for hours after lens removal has a cornea being reshaped. Recheck keratometry against the original readings before assuming the spectacle prescription has changed.

4. The one that cannot wait

Contact lens wear, particularly if extended, is the most important risk factor for bacterial keratitis. Corneal epithelial compromise secondary to hypoxia and minor trauma is thought to be important, as is bacterial adherence to the lens surface. Wearers of soft lenses are at higher risk than those of rigid gas permeable and other types. Kanski 8e, p. 175

Lower risk is not no riskRGP wearers are at lower risk than soft lens wearers — but a rigid lens wearer with a red, painful eye and an infiltrate is managed exactly the same way: remove the lens, do not patch, do not steroid, keep the lens and case for culture, refer the same day.

Check yourself

  • What does IACLE say about the oxygen performance of lenses historically marketed?
  • List four signs of corneal hypoxia in a lens wearer.
  • Why is any new corneal blood vessel abnormal?
  • Explain the mechanism of 3 and 9 o'clock staining.
  • A patient cannot see through their spectacles after removing RGPs. What is happening and what do you check?
  • Are RGP or soft lens wearers at higher risk of bacterial keratitis?
  • State the five immediate actions on finding an infiltrate in a lens wearer.
