Dry Eye Disease

Tear film structure and the two mechanisms that fail; how to classify what you are looking at; the four bedside tests and the order they must be done in; and what treatment is actually reaching for.

WCO competency mapping Category 2 — Visual Function Services (ocular surface assessment, tear film evaluation) with entry into Category 3 — Ocular Diagnostic Services (recognition and referral of ocular surface disease). At diploma level you are expected to perform and interpret the diagnostic tests and manage uncomplicated evaporative disease, and to recognise and refer Sjögren syndrome, corneal melting and infection.

1. Definitions you must be able to state

Dry eye occurs when there is inadequate tear volume or function, resulting in an unstable tear film and ocular surface disease. It is extremely common, particularly in postmenopausal women and the elderly. Kanski 8e, p. 120

Four terms are examined constantly and are not interchangeable:

TermPrecise meaning
Keratoconjunctivitis sicca (KCS) Any eye with some degree of dryness
Xerophthalmia Dry eye associated with vitamin A deficiency
Xerosis Extreme dryness and keratinisation in eyes with severe conjunctival cicatrisation
Sjögren syndrome An autoimmune inflammatory disease of which dry eye is a feature

All four definitions: Kanski 8e, p. 120

2. The tear film — three layers

The tear film has three layers, each from a different gland. Knowing which gland makes which layer is what lets you reason from a sign back to a cause. Kanski 8e, p. 120, Fig. 4.1

Lipid layer

  • Outer layer from the meibomian glands. A polar phase of phospholipids sits against the aqueous-mucin phase; a non-polar phase contains waxes, cholesterol esters and triglycerides.
  • Functions: prevents evaporation of the aqueous layer and maintains tear film thickness; acts as a surfactant so the film spreads.
  • Deficiency results in evaporative dry eye.
  • Blinking releases lipid. Thickness is increased by forced blinking and reduced by infrequent blinking.

Kanski 8e, p. 120

Why this matters at the slit lamp "Reduced by infrequent blinking" is the entire mechanism behind screen-related dry eye. A patient at a monitor blinks less, lipid release falls, evaporation rises. Your advice to blink deliberately is not folklore — it is this paragraph.

Aqueous layer

  • Main lacrimal glands produce about 95% of the aqueous component; the accessory glands of Krause and Wolfring produce the remainder.
  • Secretion has a basic (resting) and a much greater reflex component.
  • Reflex secretion responds to corneal and conjunctival sensory stimulation, tear break-up and ocular inflammation, and is mediated via the fifth cranial nerve.
  • It is reduced by topical anaesthesia and falls during sleep. Secretion can increase 500% in response to injury.

Kanski 8e, p. 120

Mucous layer

  • Secreted principally by conjunctival goblet cells.
  • As many as a hundred distinct proteins have been identified in the tear film overall.

Kanski 8e, p. 120

3. Clinical signs — what you will actually see

Tear meniscus

The marginal tear meniscus (strip) is a crude measure of aqueous volume. In the normal eye the meniscus is 0.2–0.4 mm in height; in dry eye it becomes thin or absent. Kanski 8e, p. 124, Fig. 4.5B

Corneal signs

  • Punctate epithelial erosions — stain well with fluorescein. Fig. 4.6A, B
  • Filaments — strands of mucus and debris such as shed epithelial cells, typically attached at one end to the corneal surface. They stain well with rose Bengal but less so with fluorescein. Fig. 4.6C
  • Mucous plaques — semi-transparent, white-to-grey, often slightly elevated lesions of varying size, in severe dry eye. Figs 4.6D, E

Kanski 8e, p. 124

Examination point Filaments stain with rose Bengal but only weakly with fluorescein. If you use fluorescein alone you will under-call filamentary keratitis.

Complications — these are the referral triggers

Complications can be vision-threatening: corneal melting, perforation with iris plugging, and bacterial infection. Kanski 8e, p. 126, Fig. 4.7

Refer same day Any dry eye with corneal thinning, an epithelial defect that is not closing, or a white infiltrate is no longer a dispensing problem. Melting and perforation appear in this disease and they move quickly.

4. Diagnostic procedures

Order of testing — this is procedural, not optional Anything you instil changes the next test. Run the sequence least-invasive first: symptom questionnaire → tear meniscus (observation only) → TBUT → ocular surface staining → Schirmer last. Putting anaesthetic or a paper strip in first invalidates the break-up time you record afterwards.

Schirmer test

A useful assessment of aqueous tear production. The test measures the amount of wetting of a special no. 41 Whatman filter paper, 5 mm wide and 35 mm long. It can be performed with or without topical anaesthesia. Kanski 8e, p. 124

VariantAnaestheticIn theory measures
Schirmer 1Without Maximum basic plus reflex secretion
Schirmer 2With Basic secretion
The caveat the textbook insists on "In practice, however, topical anaesthesia cannot abolish all sensory and psychological stimuli for reflex secretion." Kanski 8e, p. 124 So do not report a Schirmer 2 as a pure measure of basic secretion — it is not.

Tear film break-up time (TBUT)

Measured in a fluorescein-stained tear film; numerous dry spots appearing early indicate instability. Kanski 8e, p. 126, Fig. 4.8A

Note the textbook's warning that early break-up may reflect an epithelial surface abnormality (for example basement membrane disease) rather than an intrinsic instability of the tear film itself. Kanski 8e, p. 124 A short TBUT is a finding, not a diagnosis.

Ocular surface staining

Corneal and conjunctival staining with rose Bengal demonstrates devitalised epithelium and mucus. Kanski 8e, p. 126, Fig. 4.8C

5. Treatment strategy

Kanski organises management under these headings 8e, pp. 127–129:

  • Tear substitutes p. 127
  • Punctal occlusion p. 128
  • Anti-inflammatory agents p. 129
  • Contact lenses p. 129
  • Optimisation of environmental humidity p. 129
Diploma-level scope Tear substitutes, lid hygiene, environmental and blink advice sit squarely within your scope. Punctal occlusion and anti-inflammatory agents are prescriber decisions in most jurisdictions — know the indications so your referral is useful, and check your own national scope of practice.

6. What is happening now

Kanski 8e was published in 2016. The definitions and procedures above remain current, but the classification and treatment landscape has moved. Treat this section as signposting, not as textbook-settled fact.

Classification has been restructured

The TFOS DEWS II report (2017) redefined dry eye around tear film homeostasis and made evaporative versus aqueous-deficient a spectrum rather than two boxes, with meibomian gland dysfunction recognised as the most common driver. The layer-and-gland reasoning you learned above is exactly what that classification runs on.

Newer treatment options in active trials

Agent / deviceMechanismExample trial
Perfluorohexyloctane (Miebo) Semifluorinated alkane forming an anti-evaporative surface layer — targets evaporative loss directly NCT06565650 — Phase 4, 216 participants, corneal endothelial cell density over 12 months
Varenicline nasal spray (Tyrvaya) Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor agonist stimulating the trigeminal parasympathetic pathway — pharmacological use of the reflex secretion route described on p. 120 NCT07606625 — Phase 4, UC Berkeley, tear film stability and ocular discomfort
Lifitegrast LFA-1 antagonist — anti-inflammatory, targeting the inflammatory cycle NCT06879782 — Phase 3, 820 participants
Ciclosporin A 0.1% T-cell modulation of the ocular surface immune response NCT06898853 — UNSW, innate and adaptive immune response over 12 weeks

ClinicalTrials.gov, retrieved 20 September 2026. 157 dry eye trials were recruiting at that date.

The through-line Notice that varenicline works by driving reflex secretion — the fifth-nerve pathway Kanski describes on p. 120 — and perfluorohexyloctane works by replacing the lipid barrier function described in the same paragraph. New drugs are acting on the same three layers. Learn the physiology and the pipeline explains itself.

Check yourself

  • Distinguish xerophthalmia from xerosis. Which one implies a nutritional cause?
  • Which gland secretes each of the three tear film layers, and which deficiency produces evaporative dry eye?
  • What is the normal tear meniscus height, and what does a thin meniscus tell you?
  • Give the dimensions and grade of the Schirmer filter paper.
  • Why can a Schirmer 2 not be reported as a pure measure of basic secretion?
  • You record a TBUT of 4 seconds. Name one cause other than an unstable tear film.
  • Which stain shows filaments best, and why does fluorescein alone mislead?
  • List the three vision-threatening complications that require urgent referral.
