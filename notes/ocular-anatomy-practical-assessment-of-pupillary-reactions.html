Assessment of Pupillary Reactions

Step-by-step clinical examination protocol, patient instructions, diagnostic interpretation, and practical optometry testing pearls.

1. Purpose of the Assessment

Neurological Screening: To evaluate the functional integrity of the afferent visual pathway (retina and optic nerve) and the efferent motor pathway (oculomotor nerve, sympathetic/parasympathetic systems, and iris muscles),,.

Detect Pathology: To identify conditions such as optic nerve disease (via the Relative Afferent Pupillary Defect or RAPD test), third nerve palsies, Horner’s syndrome, or Adie’s tonic pupil,,.

Establish Baseline: To check pupil size and reactivity before instilling any drops (mydriatics or anesthetics), as drugs will alter the findings,,.

2. Equipment Required

Bright Penlight or Transilluminator: A focused, bright light source is essential, especially for detecting subtle defects,.

Distance Fixation Target: A non-accommodative target (e.g., a large letter "E" or a spot of light on a distant chart),.

Pupil Gauge / Millimeter Ruler: To measure pupil diameter. This is often found on a near vision card or a specific PD ruler,.

Near Target: Required only if checking the near response (e.g., patient's thumb or a fixation stick),.

3. Detailed Procedure

Part A: Preparation and Static Evaluation (Size & Shape)

Lighting: Dim the room lights. The illumination should be low enough to dilate the pupils but bright enough for you to clearly see them,,.

Patient Instruction: Ask the patient to remove their glasses and look at a distant target (at least 20 feet/6 meters away). This prevents the "near triad" (accommodation, convergence, and pupil constriction) from artificially shrinking the pupils,,.

Observation:

Size: Shine your light from below the patient’s nose (indirectly) to illuminate the face without triggering a light reflex. Measure the diameter of both pupils in millimeters,.

Shape: Check if the pupils are round, irregular, or peaked,.

Anisocoria Check: Compare the pupil sizes. A difference of <1 mm is often physiological. If unequal, measure them in both dim and bright light to determine if the problem is sympathetic (difference greater in dim light) or parasympathetic (difference greater in bright light),,.

Part B: Direct and Consensual Light Reflexes

Activity: Testing the constriction response to light.

Positioning: Hold the penlight 5–10 cm from the eye. Approach from the inferior temporal side or slightly off-center to avoid stimulating the near response (accommodation),.

Direct Reflex (Right Eye): Shine the light directly into the right pupil. Observe its speed and amount of constriction. Grading is often done on a scale of 0 (no response) to 4+ (brisk/very active),,.

Consensual Reflex (Left Eye): While keeping the light on the right pupil, observe the left pupil. It should constrict equally and simultaneously with the right pupil,,.

Repeat: Perform the same steps for the left eye (shine in left for direct response; observe right for consensual response),.

Part C: The Swinging Flashlight Test (Testing for RAPD)

Activity: Checking for a Relative Afferent Pupillary Defect (Marcus Gunn Pupil).

Setup: Ensure the patient is still fixating at distance in a dim room,.

The Swing: Shine the light into the right eye and hold it there for 2–5 seconds, noting the constriction,.

The Switch: Rapidly move the light across the bridge of the nose to the left eye. Place it on the left eye for 2–5 seconds,,.

Observation: Watch the pupil of the eye currently illuminated.

Normal: The pupil stays constricted or constricts slightly more,.

Abnormal (RAPD): The pupil dilates when the light is moved onto it. This happens because the brain thinks the light has become "dimmer" due to a defect in that eye's optic nerve compared to the healthy eye,,.

Cycles: Repeat the swing back and forth 2–3 times to confirm consistency,.

Part D: The Near Response (Accommodative Reflex)

Note: This is usually only performed if the light reflexes are absent or sluggish (Light-Near Dissociation).

Target: Hold a target (e.g., patient's thumb or fixation stick) about 10–40 cm from the eyes,.

Action: Ask the patient to look at the distance target, then switch focus to the near target,.

Observation: Watch for pupillary constriction as the eyes converge and accommodate,.

4. Method to Document (Recording)

Findings are typically recorded using the acronym PERRL or PERRLA, along with specific measurements.

Size: Record the diameter of each pupil (OD/OS) in millimeters (e.g., 4mm/4mm),.

Shape: Note if "Round" or "Irregular",.

Reaction: Grade the briskness of the response (e.g., 0 to 4+),.

RAPD: Explicitly state if an RAPD is present (+ve) or absent (-ve),.

Acronyms:

PERRL: Pupils Equal, Round, Respond to Light,.

PERRLA: Adds ...Accommodation (use only if near response was tested).

Example Recording 1 (Normal):

Pupils: 4mm / 4mm (dim), 3mm / 3mm (bright).

PERRL, (-) RAPD.

Example Recording 2 (Abnormal):

Pupils: 6mm OD / 3mm OS (Anisocoria greater in bright light).

OD sluggish reaction to light, OS brisk.

(+) RAPD OD.

Clinical Tip Always ensure proper patient positioning, steady fixation target alignment, and clear patient communication before initiating this procedure.
