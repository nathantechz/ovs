Lacrimal Drainage Patency (Fluorescein Dye Disappearance) test

Step-by-step clinical examination protocol, patient instructions, diagnostic interpretation, and practical optometry testing pearls.

1. Purpose of the Test

Assess Patency: To evaluate the patency of the lacrimal drainage system (from the punctum to the nasolacrimal duct).

Evaluate Tear Turnover: To estimate the rate at which new tears replace existing tears.

Differentiate Tearing Causes: To help differentiate between true nasolacrimal obstruction (epiphora) and paradoxical reflex tearing caused by dry eye or ocular surface irritation.

Detect Asymmetry: To identify unilateral obstructions by comparing the drainage rate between the two eyes.

2. Equipment Required

Sodium Fluorescein Strips: To stain the tear film.

Sterile Saline Solution: To moisten the strips.

Slit Lamp Biomicroscope (with Cobalt Blue Filter): Or a Burton lamp (UV light) for observation.

Tissues: For the patient to wipe their cheek (not their eye).

Timer/Watch: To monitor the 5-minute duration.

3. Detailed Procedure

Preparation

History Check: Assess if the tearing might be due to lid abnormalities (e.g., ectropion) or reflex tearing. Ask about any history of facial trauma or nasal surgery.

Patient Instruction: Explain the procedure to the patient and obtain informed consent. Instruct them to blink normally but not to squeeze their eyes shut, as this can artificially pump tears out.

Nasal Clearing: Ask the patient to blow their nose and clean it thoroughly with tissues before starting.

Instillation

Application: Instill equal amounts of fluorescein into both eyes simultaneously or in immediate succession.

Wet the strip with sterile saline and touch it to the inferior bulbar or tarsal conjunctiva.

Note: Using equal amounts is critical for comparing the two eyes.

Observation Period

Duration: Observe the patient for 5 minutes.

Precaution: Do not allow the patient to blot or wipe their eyes during this period, as this can wick dye out of the conjunctival sac and alter the results. You may wipe away excess dye that has spilled onto the cheek, but do not touch the lid margins.

Evaluation

Compare Menisci: After 5 minutes, examine the eyes (using the cobalt blue filter if available) and compare the height of the fluorescein-stained tear meniscus in the left eye versus the right eye.

Assess Disappearance: Observe if the dye has disappeared into the drainage system or if it remains pooling in the eye.

4. Interpretation of Results

Normal: The relative heights of the tear menisci are equal in both eyes, and the dye clears (disappears) from the tear film within 5 minutes.

Abnormal (Obstruction):

Asymmetry: If one eye has a significantly larger/higher meniscus than the other, it implies impaired drainage on that side.

Prolonged Retention: If the dye persists in one or both eyes after 5 minutes while the other clears, it suggests a blockage or stenosis in the nasolacrimal system (puncta, canaliculi, sac, or duct) or a failure of the lacrimal pump mechanism.

Note: If this test suggests a blockage (no disappearance), further testing like the Jones 1 Test (checking for dye in the nose) or Dilation and Irrigation (D&I) is usually indicated.

5. Documentation (Recording Method)

Meniscus Height: Record whether the tear meniscus heights are equal or unequal.

Flow: Note if the dye disappears into the drainage system or overflows (runs down the cheek).

Symmetry/Speed: Record the relative speed of disappearance (e.g., "OU cleared in 5 mins" or "OD delayed clearance compared to OS").

Anatomical Factors: It is also helpful to record the completeness of the blink and the position of the lids/puncta (e.g., "Puncta apposed").

Example Recording:

Fluorescein Dye Disappearance: Normal/Equal disappearance OU at 5 min.

Fluorescein Dye Disappearance: Asymmetric. OD meniscus higher than OS; dye retained OD at 5 min (suggests OD obstruction).

Clinical Tip Always ensure proper patient positioning, steady fixation target alignment, and clear patient communication before initiating this procedure.
