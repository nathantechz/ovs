Strabismus · Week 1 of 12

Introduction, Definitions, Epidemiology, and Classification.

Course Module 1: Foundations and Etiology

I. Core Definitions

1. Binocular Vision Anomalies

  • Definition: Anomalies occur when there is a problem in the coordinated use of the eyes as a pair.
  • Categories: The classification starts with two fundamental distinctions:
  • Comitant vs. Incomitant: whether the angle of deviation remains the same in different positions of gaze.
  • Strabismic (Heterotropia) vs. Heterophoric (Latent): whether the deviation is manifest or latent.

2. Strabismus (Heterotropia/Squint)

  • Definition: Occurs when the visual axes are misaligned, meaning the line of sight of one eye does not fall on the object of regard. Ideally, visual axes intersect at the object of regard; in strabismus, they are misaligned by an amount too great to allow sensory fusion within Panum’s fusional areas.
  • Terminology: The terms "squint" or "heterotropia" are synonymous, though "squint" can sometimes be confused by patients as "narrowing the eyes".
  • Microtropia: A specific subclass where the visual axes are misaligned by less than 10 prism diopters (10\Delta), often termed "microstrabismus" or "monofixation syndrome".

3. Heterophoria (Latent Deviation)

  • Definition: A tendency for the eyes to move out of alignment when one eye is covered or dissociated. It is a normal finding and is not present under normal viewing conditions.
  • Mechanism: In the absence of visual stimulus (e.g., total darkness), eyes adopt a "resting position" (tonic vergence). Distance vision requires divergence from this rest, and near vision requires convergence. Phoria represents the reversion toward this resting position when fusion is broken.
  • Compensation: Phorias are classified as compensated (asymptomatic) or decompensated (causing symptoms/breaking down).

4. Amblyopia

  • Definition: A visual loss resulting from an impediment or disturbance to the normal development of vision.
  • Criteria: Typically diagnosed when there is a difference of two lines or more between the eyes, or acuity less than 6/9, assuming no pathology accounts for the loss.
  • Critical Period: This condition is linked to the "sensitive" or "critical" period of development (birth to approx. 8–10 years), during which the visual cortex is highly plastic and susceptible to abnormal visual experience.

II. Epidemiology and Prevalence

  • General Binocular Anomalies: Affect approximately 5% of patients consulting primary eyecare practitioners.
  • Strabismus: Affects approximately 2.5% to 4% of the population.
  • Incomitant Deviations: Affect about 0.5% of the population.
  • Amblyopia: Affects approximately 3% of the population.
  • Infant Alignment: Infants are rarely born with straight eyes. Approximately 40% of newborns appear orthotropic, 33% exotropic, and 3% esotropic. Alignment is variable in the first few months.

III. Classification of Deviations

1. Direction of Deviation

  • Eso-: Visual axes turn inward.
  • Exo-: Visual axes turn outward.
  • Hyper-: Visual axes turn upward.
  • Hypo-: Visual axes turn downward.
  • Cyclo-: Cyclorotation (torsional deviation).

2. Comitance

  • Comitant (Concomitant): The angle of deviation remains constant in all positions of gaze and regardless of which eye is fixing.
  • Incomitant: The deviation varies in different positions of gaze or depends on which eye is fixing. These are often neurogenic (nerve lesions) or mechanical (muscle restriction).

3. Amblyopia Classification

  • Functional Amblyopia:
  • Strabismic: Resulting from eye misalignment.
  • Anisometropic: Resulting from unequal refractive error.
  • Stimulus Deprivation: Caused by obstruction (e.g., congenital cataract, ptosis).
  • Organic Amblyopia: Rare; results from pathological abnormalities (e.g., retinal disease).

IV. Etiology and Behavioral Perspectives

1. The "Why" of Strabismus

  • Complexity: Simple symptoms often mask complex origins.
  • Refractive/Accommodative: Uncorrected hypermetropia can drive excessive accommodation, leading to accommodative esotropia.
  • Infantile Factors: Congenital esotropia (onset before 6 months) often presents with a large deviation (>40–60 prism diopters) and cross-fixation.
  • Sensory Obstacles: Any pathology destroying vision in one eye can dissociate the eyes, leading to sensory strabismus.

2. Neuroplasticity and Development

  • Plasticity: The visual system, particularly the cortex, remains plastic during the critical period. Abnormal input (blur or misalignment) leads to structural and functional defects in the Lateral Geniculate Nucleus (LGN) and cortex.
  • Behavioral Perspective: The syllabus introduces the concept of "thwarting experiences" in infant development as a potential etiological factor.

V. Sensory Adaptations (Introduction)

When strabismus occurs in childhood, the brain adapts to avoid diplopia (double vision) and confusion (two different images occupying the same space).

1. Suppression

  • The brain inhibits the image from the deviating eye. This can be central (foveal) or peripheral.
  • In microtropia, a central suppression scotoma is common.

2. Anomalous Retinal Correspondence (ARC/HARC)

  • A "re-wiring" of sensory correspondence where the fovea of the fixing eye corresponds to a non-foveal point in the deviating eye.
  • Harmonious ARC (HARC): The angle of the anomaly matches the angle of the strabismus, allowing a form of binocular cooperation despite misalignment.

3. Eccentric Fixation

  • A monocular phenomenon often found in strabismic amblyopia where the eye fixates with a non-foveal point even when the "good" eye is covered.

VI. Diagnostic Foundations

  • The Cover Test: The core orthoptic test.
  • Cover/Uncover: Detects strabismus (heterotropia).
  • Alternate Cover: Breaks down fusion to reveal the total deviation (latent + manifest).
  • Visual Acuity: In amblyopia, "crowded" optotypes (whole line) often yield worse acuity than single isolated letters (the "crowding phenomenon").
  • Hisory Taking: Crucial elements include age of onset (prognosis is worse for later onset/detection), family history (strabismus/refractive error), and symptoms (diplopia vs. suppression).
