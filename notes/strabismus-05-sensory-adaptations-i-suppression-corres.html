Sensory Adaptations I – Suppression & Correspondence

Course Module 2: Diagnostic Techniques (Sensory & Motor)

I. Overview: Why Sensory Adaptations Occur

In a patient with strabismus (manifest deviation), the visual axes are not aligned on the object of regard. In an adult with acquired strabismus, this typically results in:

  • Diplopia (Double Vision): The object of regard falls on the fovea of the fixing eye and a non-foveal point in the deviating eye. Because these points do not correspond, the object is seen in two different visual directions.
  • Confusion: The fovea of the fixing eye views the object of interest, while the fovea of the deviating eye views a different object. Superimposition of these dissimilar images causes visual confusion.

In children, the immature visual system is plastic and adapts to avoid these intolerable symptoms through Suppressionor Anomalous Retinal Correspondence (ARC).

II. Anomalous Retinal Correspondence (ARC)

1. Definition and Mechanism

  • ARC is a binocular sensory adaptation where the fovea of the fixing eye corresponds to a non-foveal point (often called the Zero Point) in the deviating eye, rather than the true fovea.
  • It can be thought of as a cortical "re-wiring" or "re-mapping" of retinal correspondence to match the angle of the strabismus.
  • Harmonious ARC (HARC): This is the most common form. The shift in correspondence matches the angle of the strabismus exactly.
  • Objective Angle: The actual size of the deviation (measured by Cover Test).
  • Subjective Angle: The angle of deviation perceived by the patient (usually zero in HARC, meaning they have single vision).
  • Angle of Anomaly: The difference between the objective and subjective angles. In HARC, Angle of Anomaly = Objective Angle.

2. Predisposing Factors (The "Five S's")

Factors that increase the likelihood of a child developing HARC rather than deep suppression are summarized by the mnemonic "Five S's":

  • Small angle deviation.
  • Stable deviation (constant, not variable).
  • Started soon (early onset).
  • Stralsbismus is Eso (Esotropia).
  • Sensory state (Good vision in both eyes/alternating).

3. Unharmonious ARC (UARC)

  • This is a rare condition where the angle of anomaly does not equal the objective angle, leaving a small subjective angle (diplopia).
  • It is generally considered an artifact of testing or a temporary state when the angle of strabismus has changed (e.g., after surgery) but the sensory adaptation has not yet caught up.

III. Testing Sensory Adaptations: Naturalistic vs. Dissociative

The detection of ARC depends heavily on the dissociation caused by the test. Highly dissociative tests (like the Synoptophore or prism dissociation) disrupt normal viewing conditions and are likely to break ARC down into Normal Retinal Correspondence (NRC) with suppression or diplopia. Naturalistic tests are more likely to detect the adaptation.

1. Bagolini Striated Lenses (The "Gold Standard" for Naturalistic Testing)

  • Design: Plano lenses with fine striations that produce a streak of light perpendicular to the striations when viewing a spotlight.
  • Procedure: The lenses are placed at 45° and 135° over the patient's eyes. The patient views a spotlight at distance or near.
  • Interpretation:
  • Cross (X) seen: In a strabismic patient, seeing a complete cross indicates HARC. The patient has single vision (subjective angle = 0) despite a manifest deviation.
  • Gap in streak: Indicates central suppression of the deviating eye.
  • V pattern (inverted V) or non-crossing lines: Indicates diplopia (NRC without fusion).
  • Value: It is the least dissociating test, occurring in "free space" with a normal background, making it the most sensitive for detecting ARC.

2. The Mallett Unit

  • Role: Primarily a fixation disparity test, but effective for investigating suppression and ARC.
  • Test: Uses polarized letters or nonius strips in a natural environment.
  • Interpretation: If a patient with a known strabismus (seen on Cover Test) reads the polarized letters/strips seen only by the deviating eye without diplopia, they likely have HARC. If they cannot see the targets for the deviating eye, they have Suppression.

3. Worth Four-Dot Test

  • Dissociation Level: Moderate to high (uses red/green goggles).
  • Interpretation:
  • 4 Lights: Fusion (in orthophoria) or HARC (in small angle strabismus, though less likely to hold than Bagolini).
  • 2 or 3 Lights: Suppression.
  • 5 Lights: Diplopia.

IV. Clinical Implications

  • Risk of Treatment: If a patient has deep HARC (fully adapted), correcting the motor deviation (e.g., via surgery) can result in intractable diplopia because the brain cannot immediately revert to NRC. This is known as "diplopia surprise".
  • Prognosis: Microtropia (very small angle strabismus) is almost always associated with HARC and is generally considered a stable, fully adapted condition that does not require treatment.
  • Lab Goal: In the lab, you will practice differentiating between NRC (normal alignment) and HARC (strabismus with single vision) using the Cover Test (to establish objective angle) combined with the Bagolini lenses (to establish subjective status).
  • Rule of Thumb: If Cover Test = Movement (Strabismus) AND Bagolini = Cross (Single Vision), the diagnosis is HARC.
